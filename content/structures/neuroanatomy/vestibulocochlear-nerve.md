name: Vestibulocochlear nerve (CN VIII)
type: nerve
region: neuroanatomy
subregion: Cranial nerves
taName: Nervus vestibulocochlearis
aka: [CN VIII, Cranial nerve VIII, Acoustic nerve, Vestibular schwannoma, Acoustic neuroma]
summary: Special sensory nerve for hearing (cochlear part) and balance (vestibular part). It runs with CN VII through the internal acoustic meatus, where a vestibular schwannoma causes unilateral hearing loss.
tags: [cranial-nerves, ear, special-senses]
systems: [eent, neurologic]
highYield: true
status: draft
anatomy:
  origin: >-
    **Cochlear part**: bipolar neurons of the spiral ganglion in the cochlea. **Vestibular part**: bipolar neurons of the
    vestibular (Scarpa) ganglion from the semicircular canals, utricle and saccule (see [[inner-ear]]).
  course: >-
    Both parts pass through the **internal acoustic meatus** with the facial nerve and labyrinthine artery, cross the
    **cerebellopontine angle** and enter the brainstem at the pontomedullary junction. Cochlear fibers → cochlear nuclei →
    superior olive → lateral lemniscus → inferior colliculus → medial geniculate body ([[thalamus]]) → auditory cortex
    (superior temporal gyrus). Because of **bilateral ascending connections**, a unilateral lesion **above the cochlear
    nuclei does not cause unilateral deafness**. Vestibular fibers → vestibular nuclei → cerebellum, eye-movement nuclei
    (MLF — vestibulo-ocular reflex) and spinal cord (vestibulospinal tracts).
  sensory: Hearing and balance (special somatic afferent).
  relations: Cerebellopontine angle — CN V above, CN VII in front, the cerebellum and flocculus behind, CN IX–XI below.
clinical:
  - title: Vestibular schwannoma (acoustic neuroma)
    highYield: true
    mechanism: Benign Schwann-cell tumor, usually of the **vestibular** division in the internal acoustic meatus/CPA; **bilateral → neurofibromatosis type 2**.
    presentation: >-
      **Unilateral (asymmetric) sensorineural hearing loss**, **tinnitus**, imbalance; larger tumors compress CN V
      (reduced corneal reflex, facial numbness), CN VII, the cerebellum (ataxia) and cause hydrocephalus. Workup of
      asymmetric SNHL: audiogram then **MRI with gadolinium of the internal auditory canals**.
  - title: Peripheral vertigo
    highYield: true
    mechanism: Disorders of the labyrinth or vestibular nerve — **BPPV** (canaliths in the posterior canal), **vestibular neuritis** (viral, nerve only — no hearing loss), **labyrinthitis** (with hearing loss), **Ménière disease** (endolymphatic hydrops).
    presentation: >-
      **BPPV**: seconds of vertigo with head position changes; **Dix–Hallpike** positive; **Epley maneuver**.
      **Vestibular neuritis**: days of continuous vertigo, nausea, horizontal nystagmus, normal hearing. **Ménière**:
      episodic vertigo (hours) + fluctuating low-frequency SNHL + tinnitus + aural fullness.
  - title: Central vs peripheral acute vestibular syndrome
    highYield: true
    mechanism: A **cerebellar or brainstem stroke** can mimic vestibular neuritis.
    presentation: 'The **HINTS** exam (Head Impulse, Nystagmus, Test of Skew) in a patient with continuous vertigo and nystagmus: a **normal head impulse**, **direction-changing** or vertical nystagmus, or **skew deviation** suggests a **central (stroke)** cause → MRI.'
pance:
  - "Unilateral SNHL + tinnitus → **vestibular schwannoma** → **MRI of the IACs with contrast**."
  - "Bilateral vestibular schwannomas → **NF2**."
  - "Brief positional vertigo → **BPPV** → Dix–Hallpike, Epley."
  - "Vertigo + fluctuating hearing loss + tinnitus + fullness → **Ménière disease**."
  - "Worrisome HINTS (normal head impulse, direction-changing nystagmus, skew) → **stroke**."
imaging:
  mri: '**MRI with gadolinium of the internal auditory canals/CPA** — vestibular schwannoma; MRI with DWI for suspected posterior circulation stroke (CT is insensitive in the posterior fossa).'
  ct: Temporal bone CT for trauma and bony labyrinth disease.
exam:
  testing: >-
    Whisper test or finger rub for hearing; **Weber** (tuning fork on the vertex — lateralizes to the **affected** ear in
    conductive loss, to the **better** ear in sensorineural loss) and **Rinne** (air > bone normally and in SNHL; bone >
    air in conductive loss) with a **512 Hz** fork. Vestibular: nystagmus, head impulse test, Romberg, gait,
    Dix–Hallpike for positional vertigo.
  specialTests:
    - name: Dix–Hallpike maneuver
      technique: From sitting, turn the head 45° to one side and quickly lay the patient back with the head extended about 20° over the edge of the table; watch the eyes for up to 30 seconds.
      positive: After a short latency, **upbeating torsional nystagmus** toward the lower ear that fatigues within about 30–60 seconds, with vertigo.
      significance: '**Posterior canal BPPV** on the side of the lower ear → treat with the Epley repositioning maneuver.'
quiz:
  - stem: >-
      A 55-year-old has progressive hearing loss and ringing in the left ear. Weber lateralizes to the right, and Rinne
      is air > bone bilaterally. What is the next best test?
    choices:
      - Tympanometry only
      - MRI with gadolinium of the internal auditory canals
      - CT of the sinuses
      - Dix–Hallpike maneuver
      - Reassurance; this is presbycusis
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      Weber to the right with air > bone indicates **left sensorineural** loss. **Asymmetric SNHL with tinnitus** needs
      **MRI of the internal auditory canals** to exclude a **vestibular schwannoma**.
  - stem: >-
      A 62-year-old has vertigo for 30 seconds each time she rolls over in bed. Hearing is normal. Dix–Hallpike to the
      right produces upbeating torsional nystagmus that fades in 20 seconds. What is the treatment?
    choices:
      - Meclizine for 6 months
      - Epley maneuver
      - Low-salt diet and diuretic
      - Emergency MRI
      - Oral steroids
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      This is **right posterior canal BPPV**; the **Epley canalith repositioning maneuver** is curative in most patients.
      Long-term vestibular suppressants delay compensation.
  - stem: Bilateral vestibular schwannomas are characteristic of which condition?
    choices:
      - Neurofibromatosis type 1
      - Tuberous sclerosis
      - von Hippel–Lindau disease
      - Neurofibromatosis type 2
      - Sturge–Weber syndrome
    answer: D
    explanation: >-
      **Bilateral vestibular schwannomas** define **neurofibromatosis type 2**. Unilateral tumors usually occur
      sporadically.
  - stem: >-
      Why does a unilateral lesion of the central auditory pathway above the cochlear nuclei not cause deafness in one
      ear?
    choices:
      - The auditory cortex is not needed for hearing
      - Each ear projects bilaterally above the cochlear nuclei
      - The cochlea regenerates
      - Hearing is carried by the facial nerve
      - The auditory pathway doesn't cross
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      Above the cochlear nuclei, auditory pathways ascend **bilaterally** (superior olive, lateral lemniscus), so a
      unilateral central lesion doesn't cause unilateral deafness. Unilateral hearing loss points to the cochlea, CN
      VIII or the cochlear nuclei.
  - stem: >-
      In a patient with continuous vertigo and nystagmus, which HINTS finding suggests a stroke rather than vestibular
      neuritis?
    choices:
      - A normal head impulse test
      - An abnormal head impulse test with a corrective saccade
      - Unidirectional horizontal nystagmus
      - No skew deviation
      - Nystagmus that fades with fixation
    answer: A
    explanation: >-
      In vestibular neuritis the head impulse is **abnormal** (corrective saccade). A **normal head impulse**,
      direction-changing or vertical nystagmus, or skew deviation suggests a **central (stroke)** cause → MRI.
