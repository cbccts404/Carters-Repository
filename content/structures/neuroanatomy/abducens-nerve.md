name: Abducens nerve (CN VI)
type: nerve
region: neuroanatomy
subregion: Cranial nerves
taName: Nervus abducens
aka: [CN VI, Cranial nerve VI, Sixth nerve palsy]
summary: Supplies the lateral rectus. Its long course along the clivus and through the cavernous sinus makes it the cranial nerve most often affected by raised intracranial pressure, causing horizontal diplopia.
tags: [cranial-nerves, eye]
systems: [neurologic, eent]
highYield: true
status: draft
anatomy:
  origin: >-
    **Abducens nucleus** in the floor of the fourth ventricle in the caudal [[pons]]; facial nerve fibers loop around it,
    forming the **facial colliculus**. The nerve emerges at the **pontomedullary junction**.
  course: >-
    Ascends along the **clivus**, crosses the petrous apex under the petrosphenoid ligament (**Dorello canal**), runs
    **inside the cavernous sinus** beside the internal carotid artery (the only nerve within the sinus itself — the
    others are in its wall; see [[dural-venous-sinuses]]), and enters the [[orbit]] through the **superior orbital fissure
    within the common tendinous ring**.
  motor: '**Lateral rectus** only — abduction of the eye (see [[extraocular-muscles]]). Mnemonic **LR6 SO4**.'
  relations: >-
    The abducens nucleus also contains interneurons that project through the **medial longitudinal fasciculus (MLF)** to
    the contralateral medial rectus subnucleus, coordinating horizontal gaze.
clinical:
  - title: Sixth nerve palsy
    highYield: true
    mechanism: >-
      **Raised intracranial pressure** (a "false localizing sign" — the nerve is stretched along the clivus; e.g.
      **idiopathic intracranial hypertension**, tumors), **microvascular ischemia** (diabetes, hypertension),
      **cavernous sinus** lesions (thrombosis, carotid aneurysm, carotid–cavernous fistula), trauma, and after lumbar
      puncture.
    presentation: '**Horizontal diplopia worse at distance and on looking toward the affected side**; the eye is turned in (**esotropia**) and cannot abduct; patients may turn the head toward the affected side.'
  - title: Abducens nucleus and MLF lesions
    highYield: true
    mechanism: Pontine stroke or demyelination (MS).
    presentation: >-
      **Nucleus lesion** → ipsilateral **horizontal gaze palsy** (neither eye looks to that side), often with an
      ipsilateral facial palsy (facial colliculus). **MLF lesion** → **internuclear ophthalmoplegia (INO)**: the
      ipsilateral eye fails to adduct on lateral gaze while the other eye abducts with nystagmus; convergence is
      preserved. **Bilateral INO in a young adult → MS.**
pance:
  - "Eye can't abduct + horizontal diplopia worse at distance → **CN VI palsy**."
  - "CN VI palsy + headache + papilledema in an obese young woman → **idiopathic intracranial hypertension**."
  - "CN VI is the nerve **inside** the cavernous sinus → first affected by cavernous sinus thrombosis."
  - "Bilateral INO → **multiple sclerosis** (MLF)."
imaging:
  mri: MRI brain (with MR venography in suspected IIH or venous thrombosis) for any CN VI palsy that is not clearly microvascular, or in younger patients.
  ct: CT head urgently if raised ICP is suspected; CT venography for venous sinus thrombosis.
exam:
  testing: 'Test abduction in the H-pattern; cover test for esotropia. Check fundi for **papilledema**, which points to raised ICP.'
quiz:
  - stem: >-
      A 26-year-old woman with obesity has daily headaches, transient visual blurring and horizontal double vision when
      looking to the left. Her left eye cannot abduct, and both optic discs are swollen. Which condition is most likely?
    choices:
      - Myasthenia gravis
      - Idiopathic intracranial hypertension
      - Left oculomotor nerve palsy
      - Migraine with aura
      - Graves orbitopathy
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      Papilledema with a **CN VI palsy** as a false localizing sign in a young woman with obesity suggests **idiopathic
      intracranial hypertension**. Image (MRI with MRV) to exclude a mass or venous thrombosis, then lumbar puncture
      (raised opening pressure); treat with weight loss and acetazolamide.
  - stem: >-
      A 24-year-old woman looks to the right: her left eye does not adduct, and her right eye has nystagmus on abduction.
      Convergence is normal. Where is the lesion?
    choices:
      - Right abducens nerve
      - Left oculomotor nerve
      - Left medial longitudinal fasciculus
      - Right frontal eye field
      - Left trochlear nucleus
    answer: C
    explanation: >-
      Failure of adduction on lateral gaze with preserved convergence is a **left internuclear ophthalmoplegia** from a
      lesion of the **left MLF**. In a young adult, especially if bilateral, MS is the leading cause.
  - stem: Why is the abducens nerve often the first cranial nerve affected in cavernous sinus thrombosis?
    choices:
      - It is the thickest nerve in the sinus
      - It runs through the sinus itself beside the internal carotid, not in the lateral wall
      - It has no myelin
      - It enters the orbit outside the common tendinous ring
      - It is supplied only by the sinus blood
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      CN VI runs **inside** the cavernous sinus next to the internal carotid artery, while CN III, IV, V1 and V2 lie in
      the lateral wall, so CN VI is often affected first.
  - stem: A pontine lesion of the abducens nucleus (rather than the nerve) causes which finding?
    choices:
      - Only the ipsilateral eye fails to abduct
      - Vertical gaze palsy
      - Ptosis and a dilated pupil
      - Ipsilateral horizontal gaze palsy (neither eye can look toward the side of the lesion)
      - Bilateral internuclear ophthalmoplegia
    answer: D
    explanation: >-
      The abducens nucleus contains interneurons that project through the MLF to the opposite medial rectus, so a
      **nucleus** lesion causes an **ipsilateral horizontal gaze palsy**, often with an ipsilateral facial palsy (facial
      colliculus). A **nerve** lesion affects only the lateral rectus.
  - stem: What forms the facial colliculus in the floor of the fourth ventricle?
    choices:
      - Facial nerve fibers looping around the abducens nucleus
      - The hypoglossal nucleus
      - The vestibular nuclei
      - The trochlear decussation
      - The locus coeruleus
    answer: A
    explanation: >-
      The **facial colliculus** is formed by **facial motor fibers looping around the abducens nucleus** in the caudal
      pons, so a lesion there can cause both a gaze palsy and a facial palsy.
