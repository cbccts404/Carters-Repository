name: Thoracic aorta
type: artery
region: thorax
subregion: Mediastinum
taName: Aorta thoracica (pars ascendens, arcus aortae, pars descendens)
aka: [Ascending aorta, Aortic arch, Descending thoracic aorta]
summary: The aorta within the thorax (ascending aorta, arch and descending thoracic aorta). It's the site of dissection, coarctation, traumatic injury and aneurysm.
tags: [mediastinum, vascular]
systems: [cardiovascular]
highYield: true
status: draft
anatomy:
  location: Middle mediastinum (ascending), superior mediastinum (arch), posterior mediastinum (descending).
  origin: Aortic orifice of the left ventricle, above the [[aortic-valve]].
  course: >-
    **Ascending aorta**: from the aortic valve, within the pericardium, rising to the level of the **sternal angle**.
    **Arch**: begins and ends at the sternal angle (T4/T5), passing posteriorly and to the left over the **left main
    bronchus** and the left pulmonary artery. **Descending thoracic aorta**: from T4/T5, descending on the left side of
    the vertebral bodies in the posterior mediastinum and gradually moving toward the midline, to the **aortic hiatus
    at T12**.
  branches:
    - "**Ascending aorta**: [[right-coronary-artery]] and [[left-coronary-artery]] (its only branches)"
    - "**Arch** (from right to left): **brachiocephalic trunk** (→ right subclavian, right common carotid), **left common carotid**, **left subclavian**"
    - "**Descending thoracic aorta**: posterior intercostal arteries (spaces 3–11), subcostal arteries, bronchial arteries (left), esophageal, pericardial, mediastinal and superior phrenic arteries"
  supplies: The whole systemic circulation. In the thorax, the thoracic wall, esophagus, bronchi and pericardium.
  relations: >-
    The **ligamentum arteriosum** (remnant of the ductus arteriosus) connects the arch to the left
    [[pulmonary-arteries|pulmonary artery]]; the **left recurrent laryngeal nerve** hooks under the arch just lateral
    to it. The **aortic isthmus** (just distal to the left subclavian artery, at the ligamentum) is a point of fixation.
    The descending aorta lies left of the [[esophagus]], which crosses anterior to it lower down; the
    [[thoracic-duct]] and [[azygos-vein]] lie on its right.
  termination: Becomes the abdominal aorta at the aortic hiatus of the [[diaphragm]] (T12).
clinical:
  - title: Aortic dissection
    highYield: true
    mechanism: >-
      An intimal tear lets blood enter the media and create a false lumen. Risk factors: **hypertension** (most
      important), **Marfan** and Ehlers–Danlos syndromes, **bicuspid aortic valve**, cocaine, pregnancy, and aortic
      instrumentation. **Stanford A** involves the ascending aorta (surgical emergency); **Stanford B** involves only the
      descending aorta, distal to the left subclavian (usually medical BP and heart-rate control unless complicated).
    presentation: >-
      Sudden, severe **tearing/ripping chest pain radiating to the back**. **Pulse or BP differential between the arms**
      (more than about 20 mmHg). **Widened mediastinum** on CXR. Complications include AR (new diastolic murmur),
      tamponade, MI (RCA involvement), stroke, and limb, renal or mesenteric ischemia.
  - title: Coarctation of the aorta
    highYield: true
    mechanism: Congenital narrowing, usually **just distal to the left subclavian artery near the ligamentum arteriosum**. Associated with **Turner syndrome** and **bicuspid aortic valve**.
    presentation: >-
      **Upper-extremity hypertension** with **weak, delayed femoral pulses** (radiofemoral delay), lower BP in the legs,
      claudication, and a systolic murmur (often heard over the back). CXR shows **rib notching** and the **"figure 3"
      sign**.
  - title: Blunt traumatic aortic injury
    highYield: true
    mechanism: Rapid deceleration (high-speed collision, fall). Most often at the **isthmus**, where the mobile arch meets the fixed descending aorta.
    presentation: Many die at the scene. Survivors may have a **widened mediastinum**, obscured aortic knob, depressed left main bronchus, deviation of the trachea or NG tube to the right, and an apical pleural cap.
  - title: Thoracic aortic aneurysm
    highYield: false
    mechanism: Degenerative/atherosclerotic, connective tissue disease, bicuspid valve, or syphilitic aortitis (ascending).
    presentation: Often asymptomatic. Compression can cause **hoarseness** (left recurrent laryngeal nerve), dysphagia, stridor or cough. Risk of rupture or dissection increases with diameter.
pance:
  - "Dissection: tearing pain → back, **arm BP differential**, widened mediastinum; **type A = surgery**, **type B = BP and HR control** (beta blocker first)."
  - "Coarctation: **arm HTN + weak/delayed femoral pulses**, **rib notching**, figure-3 sign; **Turner**, bicuspid valve."
  - "Deceleration injury → **aortic isthmus** tear; CT angiography."
  - "Arch branches: brachiocephalic, left common carotid, left subclavian."
  - "Hoarseness + mediastinal mass/aneurysm → **left recurrent laryngeal nerve** compression."
imaging:
  xray: >-
    **Widened mediastinum** (dissection, trauma), loss of the aortic knob contour, calcium displaced inward from the outer
    aortic wall (the "calcium sign" in dissection). Coarctation: rib notching, "figure 3" contour. A normal CXR does not
    exclude dissection. {{verify: widening thresholds (e.g. >8 cm on supine AP) vary}}
  ct: >-
    **CT angiography** is the test of choice in stable patients for dissection (intimal flap separating true and false
    lumens), traumatic injury and aneurysm sizing.
  mri: MR angiography for chronic follow-up (e.g. Marfan, repaired coarctation) without radiation.
  ultrasound: >-
    **TEE** is highly accurate for dissection and useful in unstable patients (bedside, in the OR). Transthoracic echo
    may show a dilated root, intimal flap, AR, or pericardial effusion (tamponade).
  keyViews:
    - "CT angiography of the chest"
    - "TEE (unstable patient)"
exam:
  landmarks: The arch lies behind the manubrium; its top reaches about the middle of the manubrium.
  palpation: Palpate and compare radial pulses bilaterally and radial vs femoral pulses (radiofemoral delay in coarctation).
  testing: >-
    **Measure BP in both arms** (dissection, subclavian stenosis) and in a leg (coarctation: leg pressure lower than
    arm). Auscultate for a diastolic murmur of AR and for a murmur over the back (coarctation).
quiz:
  - stem: >-
      A 58-year-old man with poorly controlled hypertension has sudden tearing chest pain radiating between his scapulae.
      BP is 180/100 in the right arm and 140/85 in the left. CT angiography shows an intimal flap confined to the
      descending aorta. What is the initial management?
    choices:
      - Emergent surgical repair
      - IV beta blocker to lower heart rate and BP
      - Thrombolysis
      - Heparin infusion and aspirin
      - Pericardiocentesis
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      An uncomplicated **Stanford type B** dissection (descending only) is managed medically, starting with an **IV beta
      blocker** (e.g. esmolol or labetalol) for heart rate and BP, then vasodilators if needed. Type A needs emergent
      surgery. Thrombolytics and anticoagulants are contraindicated.
  - stem: A 14-year-old girl with short stature and webbed neck has BP 160/90 in the arms, weak femoral pulses, and notching of the inferior rib margins. What is the diagnosis?
    choices:
      - Aortic dissection
      - Coarctation of the aorta
      - Patent ductus arteriosus
      - Renal artery stenosis
      - Pheochromocytoma
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      **Turner syndrome** is associated with **coarctation of the aorta**: upper-extremity hypertension, weak femoral
      pulses, and **rib notching** from dilated intercostal collaterals.
  - stem: After a high-speed deceleration injury, the chest radiograph shows a widened mediastinum. Where is aortic injury most likely?
    choices:
      - Aortic root
      - Mid ascending aorta
      - Aortic isthmus (distal to the left subclavian artery)
      - Aortic hiatus at T12
      - Abdominal aorta at the renal arteries
    answer: C
    explanation: >-
      The **isthmus**, where the mobile arch meets the descending aorta tethered by the ligamentum arteriosum, is the most
      common site of blunt traumatic aortic injury in patients who survive to the hospital.
  - stem: What are the branches of the aortic arch, from right to left?
    choices:
      - Right subclavian, right common carotid, left common carotid, left subclavian
      - Brachiocephalic trunk, left common carotid, left subclavian
      - Brachiocephalic trunk, left brachiocephalic, left subclavian
      - Left subclavian, left common carotid, brachiocephalic trunk
      - Coronary arteries, brachiocephalic trunk, left subclavian
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      The arch gives the **brachiocephalic trunk**, the **left common carotid**, and the **left subclavian** arteries. The
      coronaries come from the ascending aorta.
