name: Vertebral artery
type: artery
region: back
subregion: Suboccipital region
taName: Arteria vertebralis
summary: First branch of the subclavian artery, ascending through the cervical transverse foramina to join its partner and form the basilar artery. Dissection and subclavian steal are the key clinical problems.
tags: [neck, vascular, neuro]
systems: [cardiovascular, neurologic]
highYield: true
status: draft
anatomy:
  location: Deep neck, within the transverse foramina of the cervical vertebrae, then the suboccipital region and posterior cranial fossa.
  origin: First part of the subclavian artery (its first branch).
  course: >-
    **V1 (pre-foraminal)**: ascends to enter the transverse foramen of **C6** (usually). **V2 (foraminal)**: rises through
    the transverse foramina of **C6–C1**. **V3 (atlantic)**: curves around the lateral mass and along the posterior arch
    of the atlas in the [[suboccipital-triangle]]. **V4 (intracranial)**: pierces the dura, passes through the foramen
    magnum, and joins the other vertebral artery at the **pontomedullary junction** to form the **basilar artery**.
  branches:
    - Spinal (segmental) branches in the neck
    - "**Anterior spinal artery** (the two vertebral contributions unite) and posterior spinal arteries"
    - "**Posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA)**, supplying the lateral medulla and inferior cerebellum"
    - Meningeal branches
  supplies: Cervical spinal cord and vertebrae, and (via the basilar artery) the brainstem, cerebellum, occipital lobes and thalami — the **posterior circulation**.
  anastomoses: Through the basilar artery and posterior communicating arteries, the circle of Willis connects with the carotid system.
  relations: Surrounded by a sympathetic plexus in the transverse foramina. In the [[suboccipital-triangle]] it lies on the posterior arch of C1.
clinical:
  - title: Vertebral artery dissection
    highYield: true
    mechanism: An intimal tear after neck trauma, sudden neck movements or **cervical manipulation**, or spontaneously (connective tissue disorders).
    presentation: >-
      **Neck pain and occipital headache** followed by **posterior circulation** ischemia: vertigo, ataxia, diplopia,
      dysarthria, dysphagia, and **lateral medullary (Wallenberg) syndrome** (ipsilateral facial and contralateral body
      pain/temperature loss, ipsilateral Horner syndrome, hoarseness). A common cause of stroke in younger adults.
  - title: Subclavian steal syndrome
    highYield: true
    mechanism: Stenosis of the **proximal subclavian artery** (before the vertebral origin) makes blood flow **backward** down the ipsilateral vertebral artery to supply the arm.
    presentation: >-
      Arm claudication and **vertebrobasilar symptoms (dizziness, syncope) with arm exercise**. A **systolic BP difference
      between the arms** (commonly more than 15–20 mmHg) and a weaker pulse on the affected side.
      {{verify: BP difference thresholds vary}}
  - title: Vertebral artery injury with cervical fractures
    highYield: false
    mechanism: Fractures involving the transverse foramina, facet dislocations, or upper cervical injuries.
    presentation: Often asymptomatic initially; screening CT angiography in high-risk injury patterns.

imaging:
  ct: '**CT angiography** of the head and neck: dissection (tapered narrowing, intimal flap, pseudoaneurysm), stenosis, and injury after trauma.'
  mri: MRI/MRA with fat-suppressed sequences shows **intramural hematoma** (crescent sign) in dissection and posterior circulation infarcts on diffusion imaging.
  ultrasound: Duplex ultrasound shows **reversed flow** in the vertebral artery in subclavian steal.
  keyViews:
    - "CTA or MRA of the neck and head"
    - "Duplex of the vertebral and subclavian arteries"
exam:
  landmarks: The V2 segment lies deep in the transverse foramina and can't be palpated.
  testing: >-
    **Bilateral arm blood pressures** and radial pulses (subclavian steal). Cranial nerve and cerebellar exam
    (nystagmus, dysmetria, gait) for posterior circulation ischemia. Horner syndrome and crossed sensory loss suggest a
    lateral medullary infarct.
quiz:
  - stem: >-
      A 38-year-old woman develops occipital headache and neck pain 2 days after chiropractic neck manipulation, followed by
      vertigo, dysarthria and a right Horner syndrome. What is the most likely diagnosis?
    choices:
      - Carotid artery dissection
      - Vertebral artery dissection
      - Subarachnoid hemorrhage from an anterior communicating aneurysm
      - Migraine with aura
      - Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      Neck pain after manipulation followed by **posterior circulation** signs and Horner syndrome (lateral medulla)
      suggests **vertebral artery dissection**. Carotid dissection causes anterior circulation (hemispheric) symptoms.
  - stem: A 64-year-old smoker becomes dizzy while painting a ceiling with his left arm. BP is 150/80 on the right and 118/70 on the left. What is the mechanism of his symptoms?
    choices:
      - Carotid sinus hypersensitivity
      - Retrograde flow in the left vertebral artery due to proximal left subclavian stenosis
      - Aortic stenosis
      - Vertebral artery dissection
      - Orthostatic hypotension
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      **Subclavian steal**: proximal subclavian stenosis causes **reversed flow** down the ipsilateral vertebral artery
      when the arm works, "stealing" from the posterior circulation.
  - stem: Through which transverse foramen does the vertebral artery usually first enter the cervical spine?
    choices:
      - C7
      - C6
      - C4
      - C2
      - C1
    answer: B
    explanation: It usually enters at **C6** (C7's transverse foramen normally transmits only veins) and ascends through C6–C1.
  - stem: Which branch of the vertebral artery supplies the lateral medulla and inferior cerebellum?
    choices:
      - Anterior spinal artery
      - Anterior inferior cerebellar artery
      - Posterior inferior cerebellar artery
      - Superior cerebellar artery
      - Posterior cerebral artery
    answer: C
    explanation: >-
      **PICA** is a branch of the vertebral artery; its occlusion (or vertebral dissection) causes **lateral medullary
      (Wallenberg) syndrome**. AICA, SCA and PCA arise from the basilar artery.
  - stem: Where do the two vertebral arteries join to form the basilar artery?
    choices:
      - At the transverse foramen of C1
      - At the pontomedullary junction
      - At the level of the midbrain
      - At the foramen magnum
      - In the suboccipital triangle
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      The vertebral arteries pass through the foramen magnum and unite at the **pontomedullary junction** to form the
      **basilar artery**, which ends by dividing into the posterior cerebral arteries.
