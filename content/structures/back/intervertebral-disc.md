---
name: Intervertebral disc
type: joint
region: back
subregion: Vertebral column
taName: Discus intervertebralis
aka: [Disc herniation, Herniated nucleus pulposus, Sciatica]
summary: Fibrocartilaginous joint between vertebral bodies, made of a tough anulus fibrosus around a gel-like nucleus pulposus. Lumbar disc herniation at L4–L5 or L5–S1 causes sciatica.
tags: [spine, low-back-pain, radiculopathy]
systems: [musculoskeletal, neurologic]
highYield: true
status: draft
anatomy:
  location: Between adjacent vertebral bodies from C2–C3 to L5–S1 (none between C1 and C2).
  classification: Secondary cartilaginous joint (symphysis).
  bones: Vertebral bodies of adjacent vertebrae, covered by cartilaginous end plates.
  capsule: None (not synovial). The **anulus fibrosus** (concentric lamellae of fibrocartilage) encloses the **nucleus pulposus** (gelatinous, a remnant of the notochord), which acts as a shock absorber.
  ligaments:
    - "Anterior longitudinal ligament (strong, anterior)"
    - "Posterior longitudinal ligament (weaker and **narrower**, especially in the lumbar region, so herniations go **posterolateral**)"
  movements:
    - Small movements at each level that add up to the spine's overall flexion, extension, lateral flexion and rotation
  stability: Anulus fibrosus and longitudinal ligaments.
  innervation: The outer anulus is innervated by the sinuvertebral (recurrent meningeal) nerves; the inner disc is aneural and largely avascular (nourished by diffusion), so it heals poorly.
  notes: >-
    **Which root is compressed?** In the lumbar spine a typical **posterolateral (paracentral) herniation** compresses
    the **traversing root**, the one numbered for the **lower** vertebra (**L4–L5 → L5**, **L5–S1 → S1**). A **far-lateral
    (foraminal) herniation** compresses the **exiting root**, numbered for the **upper** vertebra (L4–L5 → L4). In the
    cervical spine a C5–C6 herniation compresses **C6**. A large **central** herniation can compress the [[cauda-equina]].
clinical:
  - title: Lumbar disc herniation (sciatica)
    highYield: true
    mechanism: Degeneration and tears of the anulus allow the nucleus to herniate, usually posterolaterally at **L4–L5 or L5–S1**, often after lifting or flexion–rotation. Peak age 30–50.
    presentation: >-
      Low back pain with **radicular leg pain below the knee**, worse with sitting, coughing or sneezing. Dermatomal
      numbness, myotomal weakness and reflex changes. **Positive straight leg raise** (and crossed SLR, which is more
      specific). **L4**: knee extension, patellar reflex, medial leg. **L5**: great-toe extension and ankle dorsiflexion,
      hip abduction, dorsum of the foot, no reliable reflex. **S1**: plantarflexion and eversion, **Achilles reflex**,
      lateral foot and sole.
  - title: Red flags in low back pain
    highYield: true
    mechanism: Features that suggest fracture, cancer, infection or cauda equina syndrome rather than a mechanical cause.
    presentation: >-
      Age over 50 with new pain, cancer history, unexplained weight loss, fever or IV drug use, immunosuppression,
      significant trauma or osteoporosis, night pain or pain at rest, progressive neurologic deficit, **saddle anesthesia
      and urinary retention or incontinence**. Without red flags, acute low back pain is managed **without imaging**
      (stay active, NSAIDs, heat), since most improve within about 6 weeks.
pance:
  - "L4–L5 posterolateral herniation → **L5 root**; L5–S1 → **S1 root**; far-lateral L4–L5 → L4."
  - "**L4**: patellar reflex, knee extension. **L5**: great-toe extension/dorsiflexion, no reflex. **S1**: Achilles reflex, plantarflexion."
  - "**Straight leg raise** (30–70°) is sensitive; **crossed SLR** is specific for herniation."
  - "No red flags → no imaging for acute low back pain; MRI if red flags or persistent deficits after about 6 weeks of conservative care."
imaging:
  xray: Not useful for disc herniation (discs are radiolucent). Disc space narrowing and osteophytes indicate degeneration.
  ct: CT myelography if MRI is contraindicated.
  mri: '**MRI** is the test of choice: herniation size and location, root compression, and red-flag pathology. Many asymptomatic people have disc bulges, so correlate with the exam.'
  keyViews:
    - "MRI lumbar spine (sagittal and axial T2)"
exam:
  landmarks: Iliac crests at L4 help identify the L4–L5 interspace.
  testing: >-
    Heel walking (L5, dorsiflexion) and toe walking (S1, plantarflexion). Great-toe extension (L5), knee extension (L4).
    Patellar (L4) and Achilles (S1) reflexes. Dermatomal sensation. Check perianal sensation and ask about bladder
    function when cauda equina is a concern.
  specialTests:
    - name: Straight leg raise (Lasègue)
      technique: With the patient supine and the knee straight, passively raise the leg.
      positive: Radicular pain radiating **below the knee** between about 30° and 70° of elevation (back or hamstring pain alone doesn't count). Dorsiflexing the ankle may increase it.
      significance: L5 or S1 nerve root tension (disc herniation). Sensitive but not specific.
    - name: Crossed straight leg raise
      technique: Raise the asymptomatic leg.
      positive: Pain in the symptomatic leg.
      significance: Highly specific for lumbar disc herniation.
quiz:
  - stem: >-
      A 38-year-old man has back pain radiating down the lateral leg to the dorsum of the foot after lifting a heavy box.
      He has weak great-toe extension and normal patellar and Achilles reflexes. A posterolateral herniation at which
      level is most likely?
    choices:
      - L2–L3
      - L3–L4
      - L4–L5
      - L5–S1
      - S1–S2
    answer: C
    explanation: >-
      Weak great-toe extension and dorsum-of-foot numbness with normal reflexes indicate an **L5** radiculopathy. A
      posterolateral herniation at **L4–L5** compresses the traversing L5 root.
  - stem: A patient has sciatica with weak plantarflexion, numbness of the lateral foot, and an absent ankle reflex. Which root is affected?
    choices:
      - L3
      - L4
      - L5
      - S1
      - S3
    answer: D
    explanation: >-
      **S1**: plantarflexion (gastrocnemius–soleus), lateral foot and sole sensation, and the **Achilles reflex**.
  - stem: A 34-year-old woman has 1 week of mechanical low back pain without radiation, trauma or systemic symptoms. Neurologic exam is normal. What is the most appropriate management?
    choices:
      - Lumbar spine MRI
      - Lumbar radiographs
      - Staying active, NSAIDs or heat, and reassurance
      - Strict bed rest for 2 weeks
      - Referral for surgical consultation
    answer: C
    explanation: >-
      Acute low back pain **without red flags** is managed conservatively without imaging. Staying active is better than
      bed rest; most patients improve within weeks.
  - stem: Why do lumbar disc herniations usually occur posterolaterally?
    choices:
      - The anterior longitudinal ligament is weakest
      - The posterior longitudinal ligament is narrow and weaker laterally
      - The nucleus pulposus lies anteriorly
      - The facet joints push the disc laterally
      - The ligamentum flavum is absent in the lumbar spine
    answer: B
    explanation: The **posterior longitudinal ligament** is narrow in the lumbar region, leaving the posterolateral anulus relatively unsupported.
  - stem: A far-lateral (foraminal) disc herniation at L4–L5 compresses which nerve root?
    choices:
      - L2
      - L3
      - S1
      - L5
      - L4
    answer: E
    explanation: >-
      A typical **posterolateral** L4–L5 herniation compresses the **traversing L5 root**, but a **far-lateral
      (foraminal)** herniation compresses the **exiting root**, numbered for the upper vertebra: **L4**.
