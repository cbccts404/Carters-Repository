---
name: Lateral ankle ligaments
type: ligament
region: lower-limb
subregion: Ankle & foot
taName: Ligamentum collaterale laterale articulationis talocruralis
aka: [ATFL, CFL, PTFL, Lateral collateral ligament of the ankle]
summary: Three ligaments from the lateral malleolus — anterior talofibular, calcaneofibular and posterior talofibular — that resist inversion. The ATFL is the most commonly injured ligament in the body.
tags: [ankle, sports-medicine]
highYield: true
status: draft
anatomy:
  location: Lateral side of the [[ankle-joint]], radiating from the lateral malleolus of the [[fibula]].
  attachments: >-
    **Anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL)**: anterior margin of the lateral malleolus → neck of the talus; the weakest.
    **Calcaneofibular ligament (CFL)**: tip of the lateral malleolus → lateral surface of the calcaneus (crosses both
    the ankle and subtalar joints). **Posterior talofibular ligament (PTFL)**: malleolar fossa → lateral tubercle of
    the talus; the strongest.
  function: Resist **inversion** and anterior displacement of the talus. The ATFL is taut in **plantarflexion**; the CFL in dorsiflexion.
  relations: The fibularis longus and brevis tendons pass behind the lateral malleolus over the CFL; the sural nerve runs behind the malleolus.
clinical:
  - title: Lateral ankle sprain
    highYield: true
    mechanism: '**Inversion of the plantarflexed foot** — the ATFL tears first; greater force also tears the CFL; PTFL tears are rare (dislocation).'
    presentation: >-
      Lateral swelling and bruising, tenderness in front of the lateral malleolus. Grade I (stretch), II (partial tear),
      III (complete tear; positive anterior drawer). Treatment: protection, ice, compression, elevation, early
      weight-bearing, and **balance/proprioception training** to prevent recurrence; surgery rarely.
  - title: Chronic ankle instability
    highYield: false
    mechanism: Incomplete healing and proprioceptive deficit after recurrent sprains.
    presentation: Repeated "giving way"; persistent pain may signal an osteochondral lesion of the talus or fibularis tendon injury.
  - title: Injuries mimicking a sprain
    highYield: true
    mechanism: The same inversion mechanism can fracture the **base of the 5th metatarsal** or the lateral malleolus, or injure the fibularis tendons or syndesmosis.
    presentation: Always palpate the 5th metatarsal base, both malleoli, the proximal fibula and the syndesmosis.
pance:
  - "Most commonly sprained ligament: **ATFL** (inversion + plantarflexion)."
  - "Order of failure: ATFL → CFL → PTFL."
  - "Prevent recurrence with **proprioception/balance training**."
imaging:
  xray: Only if the Ottawa rules are met — to exclude malleolar and 5th metatarsal fractures.
  mri: Chronic instability or persistent pain (osteochondral lesions, tendon tears).
  ultrasound: Dynamic assessment of ATFL and CFL integrity.
exam:
  landmarks: The ATFL runs forward from the anterior edge of the lateral malleolus; the CFL runs down and back from its tip.
  palpation: Maximal tenderness just anterior and inferior to the lateral malleolus = ATFL.
  specialTests:
    - name: Anterior drawer test of the ankle
      technique: Stabilize the tibia and pull the heel forward with the ankle slightly plantarflexed.
      positive: Excess anterior talar translation or a "suction" dimple in front of the lateral malleolus.
      significance: ATFL tear.
    - name: Talar tilt (inversion stress) test
      technique: Invert the hindfoot with the ankle in neutral.
      positive: Increased inversion compared with the other side.
      significance: CFL injury.
quiz:
  - stem: A basketball player lands on another player's foot and inverts his plantarflexed ankle. Which ligament is most likely torn?
    choices:
      - Deltoid ligament
      - Anterior talofibular ligament
      - Calcaneofibular ligament
      - Posterior talofibular ligament
      - Spring ligament
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      The **ATFL**, the weakest lateral ligament and taut in plantarflexion, is the first to fail with inversion of the
      plantarflexed foot. The CFL tears with greater force.
  - stem: What is the most important intervention to prevent recurrent lateral ankle sprains?
    choices:
      - Prolonged immobilization in a cast
      - Balance and proprioception training
      - Corticosteroid injection
      - Early surgical repair for all grade II sprains
      - Avoiding weight-bearing for 6 weeks
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      **Neuromuscular (balance/proprioception) training** reduces recurrent sprains. Early functional mobilization is
      better than prolonged immobilization, and surgery is reserved for chronic instability.
