---
name: Collateral ligaments of the knee
type: ligament
region: lower-limb
subregion: Knee & popliteal fossa
taName: Ligamentum collaterale tibiale; Ligamentum collaterale fibulare
aka: [MCL, LCL, Medial collateral ligament, Lateral collateral ligament, Tibial collateral ligament, Fibular collateral ligament]
summary: Side ligaments of the knee — the broad MCL, attached to the medial meniscus, resists valgus stress; the cord-like LCL resists varus stress. The MCL is the most commonly injured knee ligament.
tags: [knee, sports-medicine]
systems: [musculoskeletal]
highYield: true
status: draft
anatomy:
  location: On the medial and lateral sides of the [[knee-joint]].
  attachments: >-
    **MCL (tibial collateral)**: a flat band from the medial epicondyle of the [[femur]] to the medial condyle and
    upper medial shaft of the [[tibia]]; its deep fibers are **firmly attached to the medial meniscus and capsule**.
    **LCL (fibular collateral)**: a round cord from the lateral epicondyle to the head of the [[fibula]], **separate
    from the capsule and lateral meniscus** (the popliteus tendon passes between them).
  function: '**MCL** resists **valgus** (abduction) force and external rotation; **LCL** resists **varus** (adduction) force. Both are taut in extension.'
  relations: The pes anserinus (sartorius, gracilis, semitendinosus) crosses superficial to the distal MCL. The [[common-fibular-nerve]] lies close to the LCL and fibular head — assess it with lateral/posterolateral injuries.
clinical:
  - title: MCL sprain
    highYield: true
    mechanism: A **valgus blow to the lateral knee** (e.g. a football tackle) or valgus twisting.
    presentation: >-
      Medial knee pain and tenderness along the ligament; pain or laxity on **valgus stress at 30°** of flexion. Graded
      I–III. Isolated MCL injuries heal well **non-operatively** (hinged brace and rehab). Look for associated ACL and
      medial meniscal tears (unhappy triad).
  - title: LCL and posterolateral corner injury
    highYield: false
    mechanism: A varus blow to the medial knee or hyperextension–varus injury; often combined with cruciate injury.
    presentation: Lateral knee pain, varus laxity; check the **common fibular nerve** (foot drop) and posterolateral corner structures; often needs surgical repair.
  - title: Pes anserine bursitis
    highYield: false
    mechanism: Irritation of the bursa beneath the pes anserinus, common with knee OA and obesity.
    presentation: Tenderness about 2–5 cm below the medial joint line over the proximal medial tibia, below the MCL's joint-line portion.
pance:
  - "Valgus blow → **MCL**; varus blow → **LCL**."
  - "Test collaterals at **30° of flexion** (to isolate them from the cruciates and capsule)."
  - "Isolated MCL tear → brace and rehab (non-operative)."
  - "LCL/posterolateral injury → check the **common fibular nerve**."
imaging:
  xray: Rules out fractures; the Pellegrini–Stieda lesion (calcification at the medial femoral epicondyle) indicates a chronic MCL injury.
  mri: Grades collateral injuries and detects associated cruciate, meniscal and posterolateral corner injuries.
  ultrasound: Dynamic assessment of the MCL.
exam:
  landmarks: The MCL runs from the medial epicondyle across the joint line to the upper medial tibia; the LCL is palpable as a cord between the lateral epicondyle and fibular head when the leg is crossed in "figure-4".
  palpation: Tenderness along the course of each ligament and at its attachments.
  specialTests:
    - name: Valgus stress test
      technique: Apply a valgus (abduction) force at the knee while stabilizing the ankle, at 0° and at 30° of flexion.
      positive: Pain or medial joint-line gapping. Laxity at **30°** = isolated MCL; laxity also at **0°** = additional cruciate/capsular injury.
      significance: MCL injury.
    - name: Varus stress test
      technique: Apply a varus (adduction) force at 0° and 30° of flexion.
      positive: Pain or lateral gapping.
      significance: LCL (and, at 0°, posterolateral corner/cruciate) injury.
quiz:
  - stem: >-
      A football player is tackled from the side, with a blow to the lateral aspect of his planted knee. He has medial
      knee pain. Which structure is most likely injured?
    choices:
      - Lateral collateral ligament
      - Medial collateral ligament
      - Posterior cruciate ligament
      - Lateral meniscus only
      - Patellar tendon
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      A blow to the **lateral** knee produces a **valgus** force that stretches the **medial** side, injuring the MCL
      (often with the ACL and medial meniscus).
  - stem: Why is the medial meniscus injured more often than the lateral meniscus?
    choices:
      - It is larger and O-shaped
      - It is firmly attached to the tibial collateral ligament and less mobile
      - It has no blood supply at all
      - It is attached to the popliteus
      - It lies outside the joint capsule
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      The medial meniscus is anchored to the **MCL** and capsule, so it moves less and is trapped more easily. The LCL
      is separate from the lateral meniscus, which is also partly mobilized by popliteus.
  - stem: Why are the collateral ligaments of the knee tested with the knee flexed to about 30°?
    choices:
      - To isolate them from the cruciate ligaments and posterior capsule
      - Because the collaterals are slack in full extension
      - To relax the quadriceps tendon
      - To lock the knee with the screw-home mechanism
      - Because the menisci block testing in extension
    answer: A
    explanation: >-
      At **30° of flexion**, the cruciates and posterior capsule contribute less to varus/valgus stability, so laxity
      reflects the **collateral ligament** itself. Both collaterals are taut in extension; laxity in full extension
      implies a more extensive injury.
  - stem: >-
      A rugby player takes a blow to the medial side of his knee. He has lateral knee pain, varus laxity and a new foot
      drop. Which ligament and nerve are most likely injured?
    choices:
      - Medial collateral ligament and tibial nerve
      - Lateral collateral ligament and common fibular nerve
      - Anterior cruciate ligament and femoral nerve
      - Posterior cruciate ligament and saphenous nerve
      - Medial collateral ligament and saphenous nerve
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      A **varus** force (blow to the medial knee) injures the **LCL** and posterolateral corner. The **common fibular
      nerve** lies close to the LCL and fibular head, so check for **foot drop**. A valgus blow injures the MCL.
  - stem: >-
      A 62-year-old woman with obesity and knee osteoarthritis has pain and tenderness over the proximal medial tibia,
      about 3 cm below the joint line. Valgus stress is painless. What is the most likely diagnosis?
    choices:
      - Medial meniscal tear
      - MCL sprain
      - Prepatellar bursitis
      - Popliteal (Baker) cyst
      - Pes anserine bursitis
    answer: E
    explanation: >-
      **Pes anserine bursitis** causes tenderness about **2–5 cm below the medial joint line** over the proximal medial
      tibia, where sartorius, gracilis and semitendinosus insert superficial to the distal MCL. It is common with knee
      OA and obesity. MCL sprain hurts with valgus stress; meniscal tears cause joint-line tenderness.
