---
name: Menisci of the knee
type: ligament
region: lower-limb
subregion: Knee & popliteal fossa
taName: Meniscus medialis; Meniscus lateralis
aka: [Medial meniscus, Lateral meniscus, Semilunar cartilages]
summary: Two crescent-shaped fibrocartilage pads on the tibial plateau that deepen the articular surfaces, spread load and absorb shock. Tears — traumatic in the young, degenerative in older adults — cause joint-line pain, catching and locking.
tags: [knee, sports-medicine]
systems: [musculoskeletal]
highYield: true
status: draft
anatomy:
  location: On the tibial plateau of the [[knee-joint]], between the femoral and tibial condyles.
  attachments: >-
    Horns attached to the intercondylar area of the [[tibia]]; margins to the capsule (coronary ligaments); the
    **transverse ligament** joins them anteriorly. **Medial meniscus**: C-shaped, wider posteriorly, **firmly attached to
    the MCL** — less mobile. **Lateral meniscus**: nearly circular (O-shaped), **not attached to the LCL**, more mobile;
    popliteus attaches to it and pulls it backward during flexion. Meniscofemoral ligaments join the lateral meniscus
    to the medial femoral condyle alongside the PCL.
  function: Increase joint congruity, transmit and distribute load (about half of the load across the knee), absorb shock, contribute to stability and lubrication.
  relations: >-
    Blood supply comes from the genicular arteries to the **outer third (red zone)**, which can heal; the inner
    two-thirds (white zone) is avascular and nourished by synovial fluid, so tears there rarely heal.
clinical:
  - title: Meniscal tear
    highYield: true
    mechanism: >-
      **Traumatic**: twisting on a flexed, weight-bearing knee in young athletes (the medial meniscus is torn more often).
      **Degenerative**: minimal trauma (squatting) in middle-aged and older adults, often with OA.
    presentation: >-
      **Joint-line pain and tenderness**, swelling developing **gradually over a day** (less vascular than an ACL
      tear), and mechanical symptoms — **catching or locking** (a displaced bucket-handle tear can block full
      extension). Positive McMurray, Thessaly and Apley tests.
    notes: Degenerative tears are treated with physical therapy first (arthroscopy adds little). A **locked knee** or repairable peripheral tear in a young patient → arthroscopic repair or partial meniscectomy.
  - title: Discoid lateral meniscus
    highYield: false
    mechanism: Congenital disc-shaped lateral meniscus, prone to tearing.
    presentation: '"Snapping knee" in a child, sometimes with pain or locking.'
pance:
  - "Twisting injury, joint-line tenderness, **delayed swelling, locking** → meniscal tear."
  - "**Medial** meniscus torn more often (attached to the MCL)."
  - "Locked knee (can't fully extend) → bucket-handle tear → urgent ortho/arthroscopy."
  - "Outer third (red zone) can heal; inner two-thirds can't."
imaging:
  xray: Usually normal; weight-bearing films assess coexisting OA.
  mri: Test of choice — high signal reaching the articular surface on two slices; the "double PCL" sign of a displaced bucket-handle tear.
  keyViews:
    - "MRI knee (sagittal and coronal proton-density)"
exam:
  landmarks: The medial and lateral joint lines are palpable on either side of the patellar ligament with the knee flexed 90°.
  palpation: '**Joint-line tenderness** is one of the more sensitive findings for meniscal tears.'
  testing: Check whether the knee fully extends (a mechanical block suggests a displaced tear).
  specialTests:
    - name: McMurray test
      technique: Supine; fully flex the knee, then extend it while rotating the tibia (external rotation with valgus for the medial meniscus; internal rotation with varus for the lateral), with fingers on the joint line.
      positive: A painful click or clunk at the joint line.
      significance: Meniscal tear; specific but not very sensitive.
    - name: Thessaly test
      technique: Standing on the affected leg with the knee flexed 20° (holding the examiner's hands), the patient rotates the body and knee internally and externally three times.
      positive: Joint-line pain or a sense of locking or catching.
      significance: "Meniscal tear. {{verify: reported accuracy varies widely between studies}}"
    - name: Apley grind test
      technique: Prone with the knee flexed 90°; press the heel down (compressing the joint) while rotating the tibia.
      positive: Pain that is relieved when the leg is pulled up instead (distraction).
      significance: Meniscal tear (pain with distraction suggests a ligament problem).
quiz:
  - stem: >-
      A 24-year-old man twisted his right knee while playing basketball. Swelling developed the next day. He now reports
      the knee "catches" and sometimes locks. There is tenderness over the medial joint line. Which structure is most
      likely injured?
    choices:
      - Anterior cruciate ligament
      - Medial meniscus
      - Lateral collateral ligament
      - Patellar tendon
      - Posterior cruciate ligament
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      **Delayed swelling**, **mechanical symptoms** (catching, locking) and **joint-line tenderness** point to a
      **meniscal tear**, most often medial. ACL tears typically cause rapid hemarthrosis within hours.
  - stem: A 58-year-old woman with knee OA has a degenerative medial meniscal tear on MRI without locking. What is the most appropriate initial treatment?
    choices:
      - Arthroscopic partial meniscectomy
      - Physical therapy and analgesics
      - Total knee arthroplasty
      - Meniscal transplant
      - Long-leg cast
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      For **degenerative** tears without a mechanical block, trials show arthroscopic meniscectomy offers little benefit
      over **physical therapy**, which is first-line.
  - stem: Which part of a meniscus is able to heal after a tear?
    choices:
      - The outer third (red zone), supplied by the genicular arteries
      - The inner two-thirds (white zone)
      - The anterior horn only
      - The posterior horn only
      - No part of the meniscus can heal
    answer: A
    explanation: >-
      The **outer third (red zone)** is vascularized by the genicular arteries and can heal. The **inner two-thirds** is
      avascular, nourished by synovial fluid, so tears there rarely heal.
  - stem: >-
      A 19-year-old twisted his knee and now cannot fully extend it; the knee is "stuck" in slight flexion. What is the
      most appropriate management?
    choices:
      - Physical therapy for 6 weeks
      - Corticosteroid injection
      - Urgent orthopedic referral for arthroscopy
      - NSAIDs and observation
      - Total knee arthroplasty
    answer: C
    explanation: >-
      A **locked knee** that can't fully extend suggests a displaced **bucket-handle meniscal tear**, which needs
      **urgent orthopedic referral and arthroscopy** (repair or partial meniscectomy). Physical therapy first is for
      degenerative tears without locking.
  - stem: After a twisting knee injury, which feature favors a meniscal tear over an ACL tear?
    choices:
      - A pop and inability to continue playing
      - Tense swelling within 2 hours
      - Positive Lachman test
      - Swelling that develops gradually over about a day, with catching
      - Instability when pivoting
    answer: D
    explanation: >-
      The menisci are **less vascular** than the ACL, so swelling from a meniscal tear develops **gradually over about a
      day**, with joint-line tenderness and mechanical symptoms (catching or locking). Rapid hemarthrosis within hours
      suggests an ACL tear.
