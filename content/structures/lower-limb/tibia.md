anatomy:
  location: Medial bone of the leg, from the [[knee-joint]] to the [[ankle-joint]]; bears almost all the body weight transmitted through the leg.
  classification: Long bone
  features:
    - "**Medial and lateral condyles** (tibial plateau), separated by the **intercondylar eminence** (attachments of the [[cruciate-ligaments]] and [[menisci]])"
    - "**Tibial tuberosity**: insertion of the patellar ligament (quadriceps)"
    - "**Gerdy tubercle** on the lateral condyle: insertion of the iliotibial tract"
    - "**Shaft**: triangular in cross-section; its **anteromedial surface and anterior border (shin) are subcutaneous**"
    - "**Soleal line** on the posterior surface; the interosseous border for the interosseous membrane"
    - "**Medial malleolus** at the distal end; the fibular notch for the distal tibiofibular syndesmosis; the inferior articular surface (plafond) for the talus"
  ossification: Primary center in the shaft (7th fetal week); proximal epiphysis present at or near birth; the tibial tuberosity develops as a tongue-like extension of the proximal epiphysis (the site of Osgood–Schlatter traction apophysitis).
  relations: >-
    The anterior tibial artery and deep fibular nerve run on the interosseous membrane in the anterior compartment;
    the posterior tibial artery and [[tibial-nerve]] lie behind it in the deep posterior compartment (see
    [[leg-compartments]]). The **great saphenous vein** runs in front of the medial malleolus.
clinical:
  - title: Tibial shaft fracture and compartment syndrome
    highYield: true
    mechanism: Direct or twisting trauma; the most common long-bone fracture. Its subcutaneous surface makes **open fractures** common.
    presentation: >-
      Deformity and pain; watch for **acute compartment syndrome** (pain out of proportion, pain on passive stretch,
      tense compartments) — especially the anterior compartment. Open fractures need antibiotics, tetanus prophylaxis
      and surgical debridement.
  - title: Osgood–Schlatter disease
    highYield: true
    mechanism: Traction apophysitis of the **tibial tuberosity** from repetitive quadriceps pull in growing adolescents (running, jumping sports).
    presentation: Anterior knee pain and a tender, prominent tibial tuberosity, worse with activity and kneeling; self-limited, managed with activity modification and ice.
  - title: Tibial plateau fracture
    highYield: false
    mechanism: Valgus or axial load (e.g. a car bumper striking the lateral knee — "bumper fracture"), mostly the lateral plateau.
    presentation: Knee effusion (lipohemarthrosis on a cross-table lateral X-ray), inability to bear weight; CT for planning; associated meniscal and ligament injuries.
  - title: Medial tibial stress syndrome and stress fracture
    highYield: true
    mechanism: Repetitive loading in runners and military recruits.
    presentation: '"Shin splints": diffuse tenderness along the posteromedial tibial border. A **focal** point of tenderness (especially the anterior cortex, "dreaded black line") suggests a stress fracture → MRI.'
pance:
  - "Adolescent athlete with a tender bump at the **tibial tuberosity** → **Osgood–Schlatter**."
  - "Tibial fracture + pain on passive toe stretch → **compartment syndrome** → measure pressures/fasciotomy."
  - "Diffuse posteromedial shin pain = shin splints; focal tenderness = **stress fracture** → MRI."
  - "Knee X-ray fat–fluid level = lipohemarthrosis = **intra-articular fracture**."
imaging:
  xray: AP and lateral of the whole tibia including the knee and ankle. Stress fractures often don't show for 2–3 weeks.
  ct: Tibial plateau and pilon (plafond) fracture mapping.
  mri: The most sensitive test for stress fractures and bone bruises.
  keyViews:
    - "Full-length tibia/fibula AP and lateral"
    - "Knee cross-table lateral for lipohemarthrosis"
exam:
  landmarks: The anterior border (shin) and anteromedial surface are subcutaneous from the tibial tuberosity to the medial malleolus; the tibial plateau margins are felt at the joint line.
  palpation: Palpate along the shaft for focal vs diffuse tenderness; tibial tuberosity tenderness in adolescents.
  testing: Assess the leg compartments (firmness, passive stretch pain) and distal pulses after injury.
quiz:
  - stem: >-
      A 12-year-old boy who plays basketball has 2 months of pain below his right kneecap that is worse after games
      and when kneeling. There is a tender bony prominence at the tibial tuberosity. What is the most likely diagnosis?
    choices:
      - Patellar fracture
      - Osgood–Schlatter disease
      - Osteosarcoma
      - Prepatellar bursitis
      - Septic arthritis
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      **Osgood–Schlatter disease** is a traction apophysitis of the tibial tuberosity in active adolescents. It is
      self-limited; treatment is relative rest, ice and stretching.
  - stem: >-
      Six hours after a closed tibial shaft fracture, a patient has severe leg pain unrelieved by opioids and pain on
      passive plantar flexion of the toes. Pulses are palpable. What is the most likely complication?
    choices:
      - Deep vein thrombosis
      - Acute compartment syndrome
      - Fat embolism syndrome
      - Common fibular nerve palsy
      - Osteomyelitis
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      **Pain out of proportion** and **pain with passive stretch** (here of the anterior compartment muscles) are the
      earliest signs of **compartment syndrome**. Pulses are usually preserved until late, so their presence does not
      exclude it.
