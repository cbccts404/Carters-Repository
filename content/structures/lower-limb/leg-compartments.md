taName: Compartimenta cruris
aka: [Osteofascial compartments of the leg]
summary: Four tight osteofascial compartments — anterior, lateral, superficial posterior and deep posterior — each with its own muscles, nerve and vessels. Rising pressure after fractures or crush injuries causes compartment syndrome, a surgical emergency.
tags: [leg, emergency, trauma]
systems: [musculoskeletal]
highYield: true
status: draft
anatomy:
  location: The leg between the knee and ankle, enclosed by the deep (crural) fascia.
  boundaries:
    anterior compartment: Tibia (medially), interosseous membrane (posteriorly), fibula and anterior intermuscular septum (laterally), deep fascia (anteriorly)
    lateral compartment: Fibula (medially), anterior and posterior intermuscular septa, deep fascia
    superficial posterior compartment: Transverse intermuscular septum (deep) and deep fascia
    deep posterior compartment: Tibia, interosseous membrane and fibula (anteriorly), transverse intermuscular septum (posteriorly)
  contents:
    - "**Anterior**: tibialis anterior, extensor hallucis longus, extensor digitorum longus, fibularis tertius — **dorsiflexion** and toe extension; **deep fibular nerve**; **anterior tibial artery**"
    - "**Lateral**: fibularis longus and brevis — **eversion**; **superficial fibular nerve** (no major artery; supplied by fibular artery branches)"
    - "**Superficial posterior**: gastrocnemius, soleus, plantaris ([[triceps-surae]]) — plantarflexion; branches of the [[tibial-nerve]]; sural nerve and small saphenous vein lie superficial to it"
    - "**Deep posterior**: tibialis posterior, flexor digitorum longus, flexor hallucis longus (and popliteus) — **inversion**, toe flexion; [[tibial-nerve]]; **posterior tibial** and **fibular** arteries"
  communications: The compartments are closed spaces bounded by bone and non-expansile fascia — the reason pressure rises quickly when they swell.
clinical:
  - title: Acute compartment syndrome
    highYield: true
    mechanism: >-
      Raised pressure within a closed compartment reduces perfusion → muscle and nerve ischemia. Causes: **tibial
      fractures** (most common), crush injury, reperfusion after arterial repair, tight casts, burns, intramuscular
      bleeding. The **anterior compartment** is most often affected.
    presentation: >-
      **Pain out of proportion** to the injury and **pain on passive stretch** of the compartment's muscles (earliest
      signs); tense compartment; paresthesias (first web space for the anterior compartment). **Pulselessness and
      paralysis are late** — pulses are usually present.
    notes: >-
      Clinical diagnosis; measure compartment pressure if uncertain (fasciotomy if delta pressure — diastolic BP minus
      compartment pressure — is **below 30 mm Hg**). Treatment: remove casts/dressings, keep the limb at heart level,
      and emergency **four-compartment fasciotomy**.
  - title: Chronic exertional compartment syndrome
    highYield: false
    mechanism: Reversible pressure rise during exercise, usually the anterior or lateral compartment, in runners.
    presentation: Predictable aching or tightness after a set distance, relieved by rest within minutes; normal exam at rest. Diagnosed with pre- and post-exercise pressures.
pance:
  - "Compartment syndrome: **pain out of proportion + pain on passive stretch** → fasciotomy; pulses are often present."
  - "Delta pressure (diastolic − compartment) **< 30 mm Hg** → fasciotomy."
  - "Anterior compartment = deep fibular nerve + anterior tibial artery (dorsiflexion); lateral = superficial fibular (eversion)."
  - "Deep posterior = tibial nerve + posterior tibial and fibular arteries (inversion, toe flexion)."
imaging:
  xray: Identifies the underlying fracture — imaging does not diagnose compartment syndrome.
  mri: Edema of the involved compartment in chronic exertional compartment syndrome (not needed for acute cases).
exam:
  landmarks: The anterior compartment lies just lateral to the subcutaneous tibial border; the lateral compartment over the fibula; the posterior compartments form the calf.
  palpation: Palpate each compartment for tense, "woody" firmness.
  testing: >-
    Passive stretch of each compartment: plantarflex the toes/ankle (anterior), invert the foot (lateral), dorsiflex
    the ankle (superficial posterior), extend the toes (deep posterior). Check sensation in the first web space
    (deep fibular), dorsum (superficial fibular) and sole (tibial). Measure compartment pressures if the exam is
    unreliable (e.g. obtunded patient).
quiz:
  - stem: >-
      Four hours after casting of a tibial shaft fracture, a 22-year-old has severe leg pain not relieved by morphine,
      worsened by passive plantarflexion of the toes, and numbness between the first and second toes. The dorsalis pedis
      pulse is palpable. What is the most appropriate next step?
    choices:
      - Elevate the leg high above the heart and reassess in 4 hours
      - Increase opioid dose
      - Split the cast and arrange emergency fasciotomy
      - Doppler ultrasound for DVT
      - Order an MRI of the leg
    answer: C
    explanation: >-
      **Anterior compartment syndrome**: pain out of proportion, pain with passive stretch of the anterior compartment
      muscles, and deep fibular nerve numbness (first web space). A palpable pulse does **not** exclude it. Release all
      constrictive dressings and perform **emergency fasciotomy**; keep the limb at heart level, not elevated.
  - stem: Which compartment of the leg contains the posterior tibial artery and tibial nerve?
    choices:
      - Anterior
      - Lateral
      - Superficial posterior
      - Deep posterior
      - None — they run in the popliteal fossa only
    answer: D
    explanation: >-
      The **deep posterior compartment** contains tibialis posterior, FDL, FHL, the **tibial nerve**, and the
      **posterior tibial and fibular arteries**.
