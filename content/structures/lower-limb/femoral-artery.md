name: Femoral artery
type: artery
region: lower-limb
subregion: Thigh
taName: Arteria femoralis
summary: Continuation of the external iliac artery below the inguinal ligament and the main artery of the lower limb. Its groin pulse is a key exam site and the usual access for catheterization; the superficial femoral segment is the classic site of claudication-causing atherosclerosis.
tags: [thigh, vascular, peripheral-artery-disease]
systems: [cardiovascular]
highYield: true
status: draft
anatomy:
  location: '[[femoral-triangle]], then the adductor canal of the thigh.'
  origin: Continuation of the **external iliac artery** as it passes under the inguinal ligament, at the **mid-inguinal point** (midway between the ASIS and the pubic symphysis).
  course: >-
    Descends through the femoral triangle (in the femoral sheath, lateral to the femoral vein), enters the **adductor
    canal** under sartorius, and passes through the **adductor hiatus** in adductor magnus to become the
    [[popliteal-artery]].
  branches:
    - "**Superficial branches**: superficial epigastric, superficial circumflex iliac, superficial and deep external pudendal"
    - "**Deep femoral artery (profunda femoris)**: about 4 cm below the ligament; gives the **medial and lateral circumflex femoral arteries** (hip and femoral head) and **perforating arteries** to the posterior thigh"
    - "**Descending genicular artery** (to the knee anastomosis)"
  supplies: The lower abdominal wall skin, external genitalia, and the entire lower limb (with the deep femoral supplying most of the thigh).
  anastomoses: Cruciate and trochanteric anastomoses around the hip (with the gluteal arteries); the genicular anastomosis at the knee — collaterals that open up in chronic occlusion.
  termination: Becomes the popliteal artery at the adductor hiatus.
  notes: >-
    Clinicians call the segment after the profunda origin the "superficial femoral artery" (SFA) — it is an artery, not
    a superficial vessel; the "superficial femoral **vein**" is likewise a deep vein.
clinical:
  - title: Peripheral artery disease and claudication
    highYield: true
    mechanism: Atherosclerosis, most commonly of the **superficial femoral artery in the adductor canal**; risk factors — smoking, diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia.
    presentation: >-
      **Calf claudication** (reproducible pain with walking, relieved by rest), diminished distal pulses, cool hairless
      shiny skin. **Ankle–brachial index ≤ 0.90** confirms PAD. Treat with smoking cessation, supervised exercise,
      antiplatelet therapy and a statin; revascularization for limiting symptoms or critical ischemia.
  - title: Acute limb ischemia
    highYield: true
    mechanism: Embolism (atrial fibrillation) or thrombosis, often lodging at the **femoral bifurcation**.
    presentation: 'The **6 Ps**: pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, paralysis, poikilothermia (cold). Emergency — heparin and urgent revascularization; paralysis and sensory loss signal threatened limb.'
  - title: Femoral access complications
    highYield: true
    mechanism: Arterial puncture for angiography, PCI or lines.
    presentation: Groin hematoma, **pseudoaneurysm** (pulsatile mass with a bruit — ultrasound-guided thrombin injection), AV fistula, or retroperitoneal hemorrhage (high puncture above the inguinal ligament — back/flank pain, hypotension).
pance:
  - "Calf claudication → **SFA** (adductor canal) disease; buttock claudication → aortoiliac."
  - "**ABI ≤ 0.90** = PAD; > 1.3–1.4 = noncompressible (calcified) vessels."
  - "6 Ps = acute limb ischemia → emergency."
  - "Femoral pulse at the **mid-inguinal point**; vein is **medial** to the artery (NAVEL)."
  - "Pulsatile groin mass with bruit after catheterization → **pseudoaneurysm**."
imaging:
  ultrasound: '**ABI** and arterial duplex ultrasound first; duplex also diagnoses pseudoaneurysm and AV fistula.'
  ct: CT angiography maps occlusive disease and aneurysms before intervention.
  mri: MR angiography (alternative without iodinated contrast).
  keyViews:
    - "Ankle–brachial index"
    - "Arterial duplex of the lower extremity"
exam:
  landmarks: The femoral pulse is felt just below the **mid-inguinal point**, halfway between the ASIS and the pubic symphysis.
  palpation: Compare femoral, popliteal, dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial pulses on both sides; auscultate for femoral bruits.
  testing: Inspect for trophic skin changes, capillary refill, Buerger test (elevation pallor, dependent rubor) in chronic ischemia.
quiz:
  - stem: >-
      A 64-year-old smoker has cramping pain in his right calf after walking two blocks that resolves with 5 minutes of
      rest. The right popliteal and pedal pulses are diminished; the femoral pulse is normal. ABI is 0.68. Where is
      the most likely site of stenosis?
    choices:
      - Abdominal aorta
      - Common iliac artery
      - Superficial femoral artery
      - Anterior tibial artery only
      - Dorsalis pedis artery
    answer: C
    explanation: >-
      **Calf claudication** with a normal femoral pulse and reduced popliteal and pedal pulses points to the
      **superficial femoral artery**, typically in the adductor canal — the most common site of lower-limb PAD.
  - stem: The femoral head receives most of its blood supply from branches of which vessel?
    choices:
      - Superficial circumflex iliac artery
      - Medial circumflex femoral artery from the deep femoral artery
      - Descending genicular artery
      - Superficial epigastric artery
      - Popliteal artery
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      The **deep femoral (profunda) artery** gives the **medial circumflex femoral artery**, whose retinacular branches
      supply most of the femoral head.
  - stem: A patient with long-standing diabetes has an ankle–brachial index of 1.5. How should this be interpreted?
    choices:
      - Normal arterial flow
      - Mild peripheral artery disease
      - Severe peripheral artery disease
      - Noncompressible (calcified) vessels, so the test is unreliable
      - Critical limb ischemia
    answer: D
    explanation: >-
      An ABI above about **1.3–1.4** means the tibial arteries are **calcified and noncompressible**, common in diabetes
      and CKD, so the ABI can't be interpreted. An ABI **≤ 0.90** confirms PAD.
  - stem: >-
      The day after a cardiac catheterization through the right femoral artery, a patient has a pulsatile groin mass
      with a bruit. What is the most likely diagnosis?
    choices:
      - Femoral artery pseudoaneurysm
      - Simple groin hematoma
      - Inguinal lymphadenopathy
      - Femoral hernia
      - Lymphocele
    answer: A
    explanation: >-
      A **pulsatile mass with a bruit** after femoral access is a **pseudoaneurysm**, usually treated with
      ultrasound-guided thrombin injection. A simple hematoma isn't expansile or pulsatile; an AV fistula gives a
      continuous bruit.
  - stem: >-
      Hours after femoral artery access, a patient develops back and flank pain and hypotension without an obvious groin
      hematoma. What was the most likely problem with the puncture?
    choices:
      - It entered the femoral vein
      - It was placed over the femoral head
      - It was above the inguinal ligament
      - It was lateral to the femoral nerve
      - It entered the profunda femoris
    answer: C
    explanation: >-
      A **high puncture above the inguinal ligament** enters the external iliac artery, where bleeding tracks into the
      **retroperitoneum** and can't be compressed, causing back or flank pain and hypotension. Puncture below the
      ligament allows compression against the femoral head.
