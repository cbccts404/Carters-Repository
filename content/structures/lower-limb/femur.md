anatomy:
  location: The single bone of the thigh, from the acetabulum of the [[hip-bone]] to the [[knee-joint]].
  classification: Long bone
  features:
    - "**Head**: about two-thirds of a sphere, articulating with the acetabulum; the fovea gives attachment to the ligament of the head"
    - "**Neck**: joins the head to the shaft at an angle of about **125°** (angle of inclination) and is anteverted about 10–15°; a common fracture site"
    - "**Greater trochanter**: attachment of gluteus medius and minimus, piriformis and the short lateral rotators; palpable on the lateral hip"
    - "**Lesser trochanter**: attachment of iliopsoas"
    - "**Intertrochanteric line** (anterior; capsule and iliofemoral ligament) and **intertrochanteric crest** (posterior; quadratus femoris)"
    - "**Shaft**: bowed anteriorly; the **linea aspera** on its back gives attachment to the adductors, the short head of biceps and the vastus muscles; the gluteal tuberosity for gluteus maximus"
    - "**Distal end**: medial and lateral condyles and epicondyles, the adductor tubercle (adductor magnus), the patellar surface and the intercondylar fossa (cruciate ligaments)"
  ossification: >-
    Primary center in the shaft appears in the 7th fetal week. The **distal femoral epiphysis** is usually present at
    birth (a sign of a full-term fetus). Secondary centers for the head (first year), greater trochanter and lesser
    trochanter fuse in late adolescence; the distal epiphysis fuses last (about 18–20 years). {{verify: fusion ages vary by sex and source}}
  relations: >-
    The femoral vessels lie in front of the head in the [[femoral-triangle]]. The [[sciatic-nerve]] runs behind the hip,
    and the femoral artery winds around the shaft to become the [[popliteal-artery]] behind the distal femur, where it
    is at risk in supracondylar fractures.
  notes: >-
    **Blood supply of the head**: mainly the **medial circumflex femoral artery** (retinacular branches running up the
    neck), with a small contribution from the artery of the ligament of the head (obturator). Displaced intracapsular
    neck fractures tear the retinacular vessels → **avascular necrosis** and nonunion.
clinical:
  - title: Femoral neck (hip) fracture
    highYield: true
    mechanism: A low-energy fall in an older adult with **osteoporosis**; high-energy trauma in the young.
    presentation: >-
      Hip or groin pain and inability to bear weight; the leg is **shortened and externally rotated** (displaced
      fractures). Intracapsular (neck) fractures risk **AVN**: displaced ones in older adults are usually treated with
      **arthroplasty**; intertrochanteric (extracapsular) fractures keep their blood supply and are fixed with a nail
      or sliding hip screw.
    notes: Occult fractures may not show on the first X-ray → **MRI**. Surgery within about 24–48 hours reduces mortality.
  - title: Slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SCFE)
    highYield: true
    mechanism: The femoral head slips posteriorly and inferiorly on the neck through the growth plate; typically an **obese adolescent boy** (10–16 years), sometimes with endocrine disorders (hypothyroidism).
    presentation: >-
      Hip, thigh or **knee pain** and a limp; the hip **externally rotates on flexion**, with limited internal rotation.
      **Frog-leg lateral** X-ray; treat with non-weight-bearing and urgent **in situ screw fixation**.
  - title: Legg–Calvé–Perthes disease and avascular necrosis
    highYield: true
    mechanism: '**Perthes**: idiopathic AVN of the femoral head in children about **4–8 years** (boys). **Adult AVN**: steroids, alcohol, sickle cell disease, SLE, trauma, caisson disease.'
    presentation: Insidious limp and hip/groin or knee pain with reduced internal rotation and abduction. X-ray may be normal early — **MRI** is most sensitive.
  - title: Femoral shaft fracture
    highYield: false
    mechanism: High-energy trauma; in young children consider non-accidental injury.
    presentation: Deformity, swelling and pain; blood loss can exceed 1 L. Look for associated hip and knee injuries and fat embolism syndrome (hypoxia, confusion, petechial rash).
pance:
  - "Older woman after a fall, **shortened, externally rotated** leg → **hip fracture**; normal X-ray but high suspicion → MRI."
  - "Obese adolescent with knee pain and limp → **SCFE** (hip exam, frog-leg lateral); knee pain can be referred hip pain."
  - "Child 4–8 with a limp and hip/knee pain → **Legg–Calvé–Perthes**."
  - "Femoral head blood supply = **medial circumflex femoral artery** → AVN after displaced neck fracture."
  - "Long-bone fracture + hypoxia + confusion + petechiae → **fat embolism**."
imaging:
  xray: >-
    **AP pelvis and lateral hip** for hip fractures (look for disruption of the trabecular pattern and Shenton line).
    **Frog-leg lateral** views for SCFE (Klein line fails to intersect the epiphysis) and Perthes. Full-length femur
    films for shaft fractures, including the hip and knee.
  ct: Characterizes complex fractures and guides surgical planning.
  mri: Detects **occult hip fractures**, stress fractures of the neck and **early AVN**.
  keyViews:
    - "AP pelvis + cross-table/frog-leg lateral hip"
    - "MRI hip for occult fracture or AVN"
exam:
  landmarks: The greater trochanter is palpable on the lateral thigh about a hand's breadth below the iliac crest; the medial and lateral femoral condyles and epicondyles at the knee; the adductor tubercle above the medial condyle.
  palpation: Greater trochanteric tenderness suggests trochanteric bursitis/gluteal tendinopathy rather than a joint problem.
  testing: Inspect leg length and rotation; in a child with a limp, always examine the hip, including internal rotation.
quiz:
  - stem: >-
      A 13-year-old obese boy has 3 weeks of left knee pain and a limp. The knee exam is normal. When his left hip is
      flexed, it rotates externally, and internal rotation is limited. What is the most likely diagnosis?
    choices:
      - Osgood–Schlatter disease
      - Slipped capital femoral epiphysis
      - Legg–Calvé–Perthes disease
      - Septic arthritis of the knee
      - Patellofemoral pain syndrome
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      **SCFE** classically presents in obese adolescents with hip, thigh or referred **knee** pain. Obligatory external
      rotation on hip flexion and loss of internal rotation are typical. Confirm with AP and frog-leg lateral X-rays,
      and stop weight-bearing until it is pinned.
  - stem: A displaced femoral neck fracture threatens the blood supply of the femoral head mainly by damaging branches of which artery?
    choices:
      - Obturator artery
      - Medial circumflex femoral artery
      - Superior gluteal artery
      - Deep femoral (profunda) artery perforators
      - Inferior epigastric artery
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      The **medial circumflex femoral artery** gives retinacular branches along the neck to the head. Displaced
      intracapsular fractures tear them, leading to **avascular necrosis**; the artery of the ligament of the head
      contributes little in adults.
  - stem: A 78-year-old woman falls at home and cannot bear weight. Her right leg is shortened and externally rotated, but hip X-rays show no fracture. What is the best next step?
    choices:
      - Discharge with analgesia
      - MRI of the hip
      - Repeat X-ray in 6 weeks
      - Bone scan in 3 weeks
      - Physical therapy
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      With strong clinical suspicion and normal films, **MRI** is the most sensitive test for an **occult hip
      fracture**; delayed diagnosis risks displacement and AVN.
