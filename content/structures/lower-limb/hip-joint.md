summary: Deep ball-and-socket joint between the femoral head and the acetabulum, built for stability. Osteoarthritis, posterior dislocation, septic arthritis in children and developmental dysplasia are the high-yield topics.
tags: [hip, pediatrics, arthritis]
highYield: true
status: draft
anatomy:
  location: Between the head of the [[femur]] and the acetabulum of the [[hip-bone]], deep to the [[femoral-triangle]] anteriorly and the gluteal muscles posteriorly.
  classification: Synovial ball-and-socket (spheroidal) joint
  bones: Head of the [[femur]] and the acetabulum of the [[hip-bone]]
  capsule: >-
    Strong; attached to the acetabular rim and, on the femur, to the intertrochanteric line in front but only halfway
    down the neck behind. The **acetabular labrum** (fibrocartilage) deepens the socket.
  ligaments:
    - "**Iliofemoral ligament** (Y-shaped, of Bigelow): the strongest ligament in the body; prevents hyperextension, letting us stand with little muscle effort"
    - "**Pubofemoral ligament**: limits abduction"
    - "**Ischiofemoral ligament**: posterior; the weakest"
    - "**Ligament of the head of the femur**: carries a small artery (from the obturator artery), important in children"
    - "**Transverse acetabular ligament**: bridges the acetabular notch"
  movements:
    - "Flexion (iliopsoas, rectus femoris, sartorius)"
    - "Extension ([[gluteus-maximus]], [[hamstrings]])"
    - "Abduction ([[gluteus-medius-and-minimus|gluteus medius and minimus]])"
    - "Adduction ([[adductor-muscles]])"
    - "Medial rotation (anterior fibers of gluteus medius/minimus, tensor fasciae latae)"
    - "Lateral rotation ([[piriformis]], obturators, gemelli, quadratus femoris, gluteus maximus)"
  stability: The deep acetabulum and labrum, the strong capsular ligaments (tight in extension), and surrounding muscles. Most stable in extension; most vulnerable to posterior dislocation when **flexed and adducted**.
  innervation: '**Hilton law** — nerves to the muscles crossing it: [[femoral-nerve]], [[obturator-nerve]], the nerve to quadratus femoris and the superior gluteal nerve. Hence hip pain is often felt in the **groin, thigh or knee**.'
  bloodSupply: Medial and lateral circumflex femoral arteries (medial is the main supply to the femoral head) and the artery of the ligament of the head (obturator).
  relations: Anteriorly iliopsoas and the femoral nerve and vessels; posteriorly the short lateral rotators and the [[sciatic-nerve]].
clinical:
  - title: Hip osteoarthritis
    highYield: true
    mechanism: Degeneration of articular cartilage with age, prior injury, dysplasia or femoroacetabular impingement.
    presentation: >-
      **Groin (or anterior thigh/knee) pain** worse with activity, morning stiffness under 30 minutes, and **loss of
      internal rotation** first. X-ray: joint space narrowing, osteophytes, subchondral sclerosis and cysts. Treat with
      exercise, weight loss, NSAIDs, then total hip arthroplasty.
  - title: Posterior hip dislocation
    highYield: true
    mechanism: A **dashboard injury** — force along the femur with the hip flexed and adducted (the most common hip dislocation, about 90%).
    presentation: >-
      The leg is **shortened, flexed, adducted and internally rotated**. Risks: **sciatic nerve injury** (fibular
      division) and **AVN** of the femoral head — reduce urgently (within about 6 hours). Anterior dislocations present
      abducted and externally rotated.
  - title: Septic arthritis vs transient synovitis in children
    highYield: true
    mechanism: Septic arthritis is usually *Staphylococcus aureus* (Kingella in young children); transient synovitis often follows a viral illness.
    presentation: >-
      Limp or refusal to bear weight. **Kocher criteria** favor septic arthritis: fever over 38.5 °C, non-weight-bearing,
      ESR over 40 mm/h and WBC over 12,000/µL (plus CRP over 2 mg/dL). Ultrasound shows an effusion; urgent
      **aspiration** and washout for septic arthritis.
  - title: Developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH)
    highYield: true
    mechanism: Shallow acetabulum and lax capsule; risk factors — **breech presentation, female sex, first-born, family history**, oligohydramnios.
    presentation: Newborn screening with **Barlow and Ortolani** tests; later, asymmetric thigh folds, limited abduction, leg-length discrepancy (Galeazzi sign) or a waddling gait. Ultrasound before about 4–6 months, X-ray after; treat with a **Pavlik harness**.
pance:
  - "Dashboard injury, leg **shortened, adducted, internally rotated** → posterior hip dislocation (sciatic nerve, AVN)."
  - "Hip OA: groin pain and **loss of internal rotation**."
  - "Febrile child refusing to bear weight → consider **septic hip** (Kocher criteria) → aspirate."
  - "DDH: breech, female, first-born; Barlow/Ortolani; Pavlik harness."
  - "Hip pathology often refers pain to the **knee**."
imaging:
  xray: AP pelvis and lateral hip — OA, fractures, dislocation, dysplasia, SCFE, Perthes.
  ultrasound: >-
    **Hip ultrasound** detects effusions (septic arthritis, transient synovitis) and guides aspiration; it is the test
    for **DDH in infants under about 4–6 months** (before the femoral head ossifies).
  mri: Labral tears (MR arthrography), AVN, occult fractures, osteomyelitis.
  keyViews:
    - "AP pelvis + lateral hip"
    - "Infant hip ultrasound (Graf method) for DDH"
exam:
  landmarks: The joint lies deep to a point about 2–3 cm below the midpoint of the inguinal ligament, lateral to the femoral pulse; the greater trochanter is palpable laterally.
  palpation: Groin tenderness suggests an intra-articular process; greater trochanteric tenderness suggests gluteal tendinopathy/bursitis.
  testing: Range of motion, especially internal rotation with the hip flexed to 90° (reduced early in OA); gait; Trendelenburg test (see [[gluteus-medius-and-minimus]]).
  specialTests:
    - name: FADIR (anterior impingement) test
      technique: Supine; flex the hip to 90°, then adduct and internally rotate.
      positive: Groin pain.
      significance: Suggests femoroacetabular impingement or a labral tear.
    - name: Barlow and Ortolani tests (infants)
      technique: '**Barlow**: with the hip flexed and adducted, push posteriorly to try to dislocate it. **Ortolani**: abduct the flexed hip while lifting the greater trochanter forward to relocate it.'
      positive: A palpable "clunk" as the femoral head dislocates (Barlow) or relocates (Ortolani).
      significance: Developmental dysplasia of the hip.
    - name: Thomas test
      technique: Supine; the patient holds one knee to the chest (flattening the lumbar lordosis) while the other leg rests on the table.
      positive: The thigh of the extended leg lifts off the table.
      significance: Hip flexion contracture (tight iliopsoas).
quiz:
  - stem: >-
      A driver's knee strikes the dashboard in a head-on collision. His right leg is shortened, flexed, adducted and
      internally rotated. Which nerve is most at risk?
    choices:
      - Femoral nerve
      - Obturator nerve
      - Sciatic nerve
      - Superior gluteal nerve
      - Lateral femoral cutaneous nerve
    answer: C
    explanation: >-
      This is a **posterior hip dislocation**. The femoral head is driven backward toward the **sciatic nerve**
      (especially its fibular division, causing foot drop). Urgent reduction also reduces the risk of AVN.
  - stem: >-
      A 3-year-old has a temperature of 39.2 °C and refuses to bear weight on the right leg. ESR is 55 mm/h and WBC is
      15,000/µL. Ultrasound shows a hip effusion. What is the most appropriate next step?
    choices:
      - NSAIDs and follow-up in 1 week
      - Urgent joint aspiration
      - MRI of the lumbar spine
      - Pavlik harness
      - Oral antibiotics at home
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      The child meets all four **Kocher criteria** (fever, non-weight-bearing, high ESR, high WBC), so septic arthritis
      is highly likely. **Urgent aspiration** (and surgical drainage) plus IV antibiotics are needed to prevent joint
      destruction.
  - stem: Which ligament of the hip is the strongest and prevents hyperextension?
    choices:
      - Pubofemoral
      - Ischiofemoral
      - Iliofemoral
      - Ligament of the head of the femur
      - Transverse acetabular
    answer: C
    explanation: >-
      The Y-shaped **iliofemoral ligament** is the strongest ligament in the body and resists hyperextension, which
      lets us stand upright with minimal muscle work.
