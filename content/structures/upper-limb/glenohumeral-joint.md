---
name: Glenohumeral joint
type: joint
region: upper-limb
subregion: Pectoral & scapular region
taName: Articulatio humeri
aka: [Shoulder joint]
summary: Ball-and-socket joint between the humeral head and the glenoid cavity. It is the most mobile joint in the body and the most frequently dislocated major joint.
tags: [shoulder, rotator-cuff]
systems: [musculoskeletal]
highYield: true
status: draft
anatomy:
  location: Between the head of the humerus and the glenoid cavity of the scapula, beneath the deltoid and coracoacromial arch.
  classification: Synovial ball-and-socket (multiaxial) joint.
  bones: >-
    Head of the [[humerus]] with the glenoid cavity of the [[scapula]]. The glenoid is shallow, covering only about a
    quarter to a third of the head, and is deepened by the fibrocartilaginous **glenoid labrum**.
  capsule: >-
    Loose fibrous capsule from the margin of the glenoid to the **anatomical neck** of the humerus (extending further
    down the shaft inferiorly). It is **weakest inferiorly**, where there is no rotator cuff support. The long head of
    [[biceps-brachii]] passes through the joint (intracapsular, enclosed in a synovial sheath) to reach the
    intertubercular groove.
  ligaments:
    - "**Glenohumeral ligaments** (superior, middle, inferior): thickenings of the anterior capsule; the inferior glenohumeral ligament is the key anterior stabilizer in abduction–external rotation"
    - "**Coracohumeral ligament**: coracoid base → greater tubercle; strengthens the superior capsule"
    - "**Transverse humeral ligament**: bridges the intertubercular groove, holding the biceps tendon"
    - "**Coracoacromial ligament**: with the acromion and coracoid forms the **coracoacromial arch**, which prevents superior displacement of the head"
  movements:
    - "Flexion–extension, abduction–adduction, medial–lateral rotation, circumduction"
    - "Full elevation combines glenohumeral and scapulothoracic motion (scapulohumeral rhythm, about 2:1 glenohumeral to scapular) {{verify}}"
  stability: >-
    Mobility comes at the expense of stability. **Dynamic** stability comes mainly from the **rotator cuff**
    ([[supraspinatus]], [[infraspinatus]], [[teres-minor]], [[subscapularis]]) and the long head of biceps. **Static**
    stability comes from the labrum, capsule, glenohumeral ligaments and coracoacromial arch.
  innervation: "Suprascapular, [[axillary-nerve]] and lateral pectoral nerves"
  bloodSupply: "Anterior and posterior circumflex humeral arteries and branches of the suprascapular artery (from the [[axillary-artery]] and subclavian systems)"
  relations: >-
    The subacromial–subdeltoid bursa lies superiorly. The subscapular bursa (anterior) often communicates with the
    joint. The axillary nerve and posterior circumflex humeral artery pass directly below the capsule.
clinical:
  - title: Anterior dislocation
    highYield: true
    mechanism: >-
      By far the most common direction. Caused by **abduction + external rotation** (with extension), e.g. blocking a
      ball overhead or a fall. The head moves anteroinferiorly to a subcoracoid position.
    presentation: >-
      **Squared-off shoulder** (loss of the deltoid contour, prominent acromion). The arm is held slightly
      **abducted and externally rotated**, and the patient resists moving it. Check the **axillary nerve**
      (regimental badge sensation). Associated injuries include the **Bankart lesion** (anteroinferior labral tear
      ± glenoid rim fracture) and the **Hill–Sachs lesion** (impaction of the posterolateral humeral head).
      Recurrence is common in young patients.
  - title: Posterior dislocation
    highYield: true
    mechanism: Classically **seizures** or **electric shock** (strong internal rotators overpower external rotators), or a direct anterior blow.
    presentation: >-
      Arm held **adducted and internally rotated**; the patient can't externally rotate. Posterior fullness and
      anterior flattening. **Easily missed on a single AP view.** Reverse Hill–Sachs lesion.
  - title: Adhesive capsulitis (frozen shoulder)
    highYield: true
    mechanism: >-
      Inflammation and fibrosis of the capsule. Associated with **diabetes mellitus**, thyroid disease and
      immobilization. Typically ages 40–60, more often women.
    presentation: >-
      Painful, then stiff, then "thawing" phases. **Loss of both active and passive ROM**, most markedly **external
      rotation**. Radiographs are normal.
  - title: Glenohumeral osteoarthritis
    highYield: false
    mechanism: Degenerative loss of articular cartilage, often in older adults or after prior instability or trauma.
    presentation: Deep shoulder pain, stiffness and crepitus, with loss of external rotation. Radiographs show joint space narrowing, an inferior humeral osteophyte and subchondral sclerosis.
pance:
  - "Anterior dislocation (≈95%): abduction + external rotation; squared-off shoulder; **axillary nerve**; Bankart + Hill–Sachs."
  - "Posterior dislocation: **seizure / electrocution**; arm adducted and internally rotated; **lightbulb sign** on AP."
  - "Adhesive capsulitis: **diabetes**; loss of **active and passive** ROM (especially external rotation)."
  - "Rotator cuff = the main dynamic stabilizer; inferior capsule = the weakest region."
imaging:
  xray: >-
    **Trauma series: AP (or Grashey), scapular Y and axillary view.** On the Y view, the head should overlie the
    center of the Y. Anterior dislocation: head subcoracoid. **Posterior dislocation:** the AP view may show a
    fixed internally rotated head ("**lightbulb sign**") and widening of the space between the anterior glenoid rim and
    the head ("rim sign"); the **axillary view** is confirmatory. Hill–Sachs lesions are best seen on an AP view with
    internal rotation.
  ct: Quantifies glenoid bone loss (bony Bankart) and Hill–Sachs size in recurrent instability; complex fractures.
  mri: >-
    **MR arthrography** (intra-articular contrast) is the reference test for **labral tears** (Bankart, SLAP) and
    capsular injury. Non-contrast MRI evaluates the rotator cuff, bone bruising and osteonecrosis. In adhesive
    capsulitis, capsular thickening in the axillary recess and rotator interval is described.
  ultrasound: Dynamic evaluation of the rotator cuff, biceps tendon, effusion and subacromial bursa. Guides joint injections.
  keyViews:
    - "AP/Grashey, scapular Y, axillary lateral"
    - "MR arthrogram for labral pathology"
exam:
  landmarks: >-
    Acromion (point of the shoulder), greater tubercle (lateral, beneath deltoid), coracoid process (deltopectoral
    triangle), bicipital groove (anterior, between the tubercles).
  palpation: Palpate the AC joint, acromion, greater tubercle, bicipital groove and coracoid for tenderness. Inspect the contour from front and back.
  testing: >-
    Active and passive ROM in all planes (comparing the two isolates capsular vs cuff pathology). Cuff strength tests.
    Neurovascular exam (axillary nerve) before and after any reduction.
  specialTests:
    - name: Apprehension test
      technique: Patient supine; the shoulder is abducted to 90° and the examiner gradually externally rotates it.
      positive: The patient becomes apprehensive or resists, fearing dislocation (pain alone is less specific).
      significance: Anterior instability.
    - name: Relocation test
      technique: Performed after a positive apprehension test. The examiner applies a posteriorly directed force to the humeral head.
      positive: Apprehension is relieved and further external rotation is tolerated.
      significance: Confirms anterior instability.
    - name: Sulcus sign
      technique: With the arm relaxed at the side, the examiner pulls the arm inferiorly.
      positive: A visible depression (sulcus) appears between the acromion and the humeral head.
      significance: Inferior or multidirectional instability.
quiz:
  - stem: >-
      A 24-year-old man is brought in after a generalized tonic-clonic seizure with right shoulder pain. He holds the arm
      adducted and internally rotated and can't externally rotate it. The AP radiograph shows a symmetric,
      rounded humeral head. What is the most likely diagnosis?
    choices:
      - Anterior shoulder dislocation
      - Posterior shoulder dislocation
      - Acromioclavicular separation
      - Clavicle fracture
      - Rotator cuff tear
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      **Seizures** cause **posterior dislocation**. The arm is locked in internal rotation, and the AP view shows the
      "**lightbulb sign**". Always obtain an axillary or scapular Y view, because posterior dislocations are
      frequently missed on AP alone.
  - stem: >-
      A 54-year-old woman with type 2 diabetes has 5 months of progressive shoulder pain and stiffness. Both active and
      passive external rotation are markedly reduced. Radiographs are normal. What is the most likely diagnosis?
    choices:
      - Rotator cuff tear
      - Subacromial impingement
      - Adhesive capsulitis
      - Glenohumeral osteoarthritis
      - Calcific tendinitis
    answer: C
    explanation: >-
      **Adhesive capsulitis** restricts **both active and passive** motion, especially external rotation, and is
      associated with diabetes. A cuff tear limits active motion while passive motion is preserved. OA would show
      radiographic changes.
  - stem: Which lesion is an impaction fracture of the posterolateral humeral head after anterior dislocation?
    choices:
      - Bankart lesion
      - Hill–Sachs lesion
      - SLAP lesion
      - Reverse Hill–Sachs lesion
      - Bennett fracture
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      The **Hill–Sachs lesion** forms when the posterolateral head impacts the anterior glenoid rim. The **Bankart
      lesion** is the anteroinferior labral (± glenoid rim) injury. A reverse Hill–Sachs (anteromedial head) follows
      posterior dislocation.
  - stem: What is the main dynamic stabilizer of the glenohumeral joint?
    choices:
      - Glenoid labrum
      - Coracohumeral ligament
      - Rotator cuff muscles
      - Coracoacromial arch
      - Negative intra-articular pressure
    answer: C
    explanation: >-
      The **rotator cuff** muscles compress the humeral head into the glenoid throughout motion. The labrum,
      ligaments and arch are static stabilizers.
