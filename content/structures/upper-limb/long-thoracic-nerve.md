---
name: Long thoracic nerve
type: nerve
region: upper-limb
subregion: Axilla
taName: Nervus thoracicus longus
aka: [Nerve of Bell, External respiratory nerve of Bell]
summary: Branch arising directly from the C5–C7 roots that supplies serratus anterior; injury causes medial winging of the scapula.
tags: [brachial-plexus]
systems: [neurologic, musculoskeletal]
highYield: true
status: draft
anatomy:
  location: >-
    Medial wall of the [[axilla]], on the superficial (external) surface of [[serratus-anterior]].
  roots: C5, C6, C7
  origin: Directly from the roots of the [[brachial-plexus]] (C5–C7), before the trunks form.
  course: >-
    Passes through the cervicoaxillary canal posterior to the other components of the plexus, then descends on the
    external surface of serratus anterior on the lateral thoracic wall, giving branches to each of its digitations.
    Because it lies on the **superficial** surface of the muscle, it is exposed during axillary surgery and to blows or
    penetrating injury to the lateral chest.
  motor: "[[serratus-anterior]] only."
  sensory: None (purely motor).
clinical:
  - title: Medial winging of the scapula
    highYield: true
    mechanism: >-
      Injury to the nerve with paralysis of serratus anterior. Causes include **axillary lymph node dissection or
      mastectomy**, chest tube placement or thoracic surgery, a direct blow to the lateral chest wall, heavy backpack
      straps and repetitive overhead activity.
    presentation: >-
      The **medial border and inferior angle** of the scapula lift away from the thoracic wall, most obvious when
      the patient pushes against a wall or presses the outstretched arms forward. The patient has difficulty raising
      the arm above the horizontal, because serratus anterior normally rotates the glenoid upward.
pance:
  - "Post-mastectomy/axillary dissection + scapula winging when pushing on a wall → **long thoracic nerve** (serratus anterior)."
  - "**Medial** winging = long thoracic nerve / serratus anterior; **lateral** winging = spinal accessory nerve (CN XI) / trapezius."
  - "Serratus anterior is needed to abduct the arm **above 90°** (upward rotation of the scapula)."
imaging:
  xray: The nerve is not visible. Chest or shoulder films exclude fractures or masses in trauma.
  ct: Not routinely used for the nerve. May show surgical changes or chest-wall pathology.
  mri: >-
    The nerve is usually too small to assess directly. Serratus anterior shows **denervation edema** (T2/STIR
    hyperintensity) acutely and fatty atrophy chronically.
  ultrasound: >-
    Can be traced with a high-frequency probe on the superficial surface of serratus anterior along the lateral chest
    wall. {{verify: whether this is routine practice varies by institution}}
  keyViews:
    - "Diagnosis is mainly clinical. EMG/nerve conduction studies confirm it and help with prognosis."
exam:
  landmarks: Lateral thoracic wall, roughly along the mid-axillary line, over the digitations of serratus anterior.
  testing: >-
    Resisted protraction of the scapula (punching forward). Observe scapular motion while the patient slowly raises
    and lowers the arms.
  specialTests:
    - name: Wall push-up test
      technique: >-
        The patient stands facing a wall and pushes against it with the arms extended at shoulder height. The examiner
        observes the scapulae from behind.
      positive: The medial border and inferior angle of the scapula lift off the thoracic wall (winging).
      significance: Serratus anterior weakness, usually from long thoracic nerve injury.
quiz:
  - stem: >-
      A 52-year-old woman is seen 4 weeks after a left mastectomy with axillary lymph node dissection. When she pushes
      against a wall, the medial border of her left scapula protrudes posteriorly. Which nerve was most likely injured?
    choices:
      - Thoracodorsal nerve
      - Long thoracic nerve
      - Spinal accessory nerve
      - Dorsal scapular nerve
      - Suprascapular nerve
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      The **long thoracic nerve** runs on the superficial surface of serratus anterior on the medial wall of the axilla
      and is at risk during axillary dissection. Serratus anterior paralysis causes **medial winging**. Thoracodorsal
      injury weakens latissimus dorsi (extension, adduction, medial rotation) without winging. Spinal accessory injury
      causes lateral winging with a drooping shoulder.
  - stem: A patient with long thoracic nerve palsy will have the greatest difficulty with which movement?
    choices:
      - Medial rotation of the arm
      - Elbow flexion
      - Abduction of the arm above the horizontal
      - Retraction of the scapula
      - Shoulder shrug (scapular elevation)
    answer: C
    explanation: >-
      Serratus anterior (with trapezius) **rotates the scapula upward**, turning the glenoid cavity superiorly. That
      rotation is needed to abduct the arm above about 90°. Scapular elevation (trapezius, levator scapulae) and
      retraction (rhomboids, trapezius) are not supplied by the long thoracic nerve.
  - stem: The long thoracic nerve arises from which part of the brachial plexus?
    choices:
      - Roots C5–C7
      - Superior trunk
      - Lateral cord
      - Posterior cord
      - Medial cord
    answer: A
    explanation: >-
      The long thoracic nerve (C5–C7) and the dorsal scapular nerve (C5) arise **directly from the roots**. The
      superior trunk gives the suprascapular nerve and the nerve to subclavius.
