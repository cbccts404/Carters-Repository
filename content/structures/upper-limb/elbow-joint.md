type: joint
region: upper-limb
subregion: Elbow & cubital fossa
taName: Articulatio cubiti
summary: Hinge joint between the humerus, ulna and radius. It shares a capsule with the proximal radioulnar joint and is the site of common overuse and pediatric injuries.
tags: [elbow]
highYield: true
status: draft
anatomy:
  location: Between the distal humerus and the proximal ulna and radius.
  classification: >-
    Synovial **hinge** joint (humeroulnar and humeroradial articulations). The **proximal radioulnar joint** (a pivot
    joint) lies within the same capsule.
  bones: >-
    Trochlea of the [[humerus]] with the trochlear notch of the [[ulna]]; capitulum of the humerus with the head of
    the [[radius]].
  capsule: Fibrous capsule, thin anteriorly and posteriorly, reinforced on each side by the collateral ligaments.
  ligaments:
    - "**Ulnar (medial) collateral ligament**: medial epicondyle to the coronoid process and olecranon; anterior, posterior and oblique bands. The **anterior band** is the strongest and the main restraint to **valgus** stress"
    - "**Radial (lateral) collateral ligament**: lateral epicondyle, blending with the anular ligament; resists varus stress"
    - "**Anular ligament**: encircles and holds the radial head in the radial notch of the ulna"
  movements:
    - Flexion and extension (humeroulnar and humeroradial)
    - Pronation and supination occur at the proximal and distal radioulnar joints
    - "Normal **carrying angle** (cubitus valgus) of about 5–15°, typically greater in females {{verify}}"
  stability: Bony congruity of the trochlea and trochlear notch, plus the collateral ligaments and surrounding muscles.
  innervation: "[[musculocutaneous-nerve]], [[radial-nerve]], [[ulnar-nerve]] and [[median-nerve]] (articular branches)"
  bloodSupply: Periarticular arterial anastomosis (collateral branches of the brachial and profunda brachii; recurrent branches of the radial, ulnar and interosseous arteries).
  relations: >-
    Anteriorly the [[cubital-fossa]] (brachial artery, median nerve, biceps tendon). Posteriorly the ulnar nerve lies
    behind the medial epicondyle, and the olecranon bursa is subcutaneous over the olecranon.
clinical:
  - title: Lateral epicondylitis (tennis elbow)
    highYield: true
    mechanism: >-
      Degenerative tendinopathy (tendinosis) of the common extensor origin, particularly **extensor carpi radialis
      brevis (ECRB)**, from repetitive wrist extension and gripping.
    presentation: >-
      Lateral elbow pain and tenderness just distal to the lateral epicondyle. Pain with **resisted wrist extension**
      (Cozen test) and with passive wrist flexion with the elbow extended (Mill test). Grip weakness from pain.
  - title: Medial epicondylitis (golfer's elbow)
    highYield: true
    mechanism: Tendinopathy of the common flexor–pronator origin (pronator teres and FCR) from repetitive wrist flexion and pronation.
    presentation: Medial epicondyle pain worsened by resisted wrist flexion and pronation. May coexist with ulnar neuropathy.
  - title: Elbow dislocation
    highYield: true
    mechanism: Fall on an outstretched hand. **Posterior** dislocation is most common.
    presentation: >-
      Deformity with a prominent olecranon (the epicondyle–olecranon triangle is disrupted). Check the **brachial
      artery**, **median nerve** and **ulnar nerve**. Associated fractures (radial head, coronoid) can form the
      "terrible triad" (dislocation + radial head fracture + coronoid fracture).
  - title: Ulnar collateral ligament injury
    highYield: false
    mechanism: Repetitive **valgus stress** in overhead throwing athletes (pitchers, javelin), or acute rupture.
    presentation: Medial elbow pain during the late cocking/early acceleration phase of throwing, reduced velocity, and ulnar nerve symptoms. Valgus stress testing is painful or lax.
pance:
  - "**Lateral epicondylitis** = ECRB; pain with resisted wrist extension (Cozen)."
  - "**Medial epicondylitis** = flexor–pronator origin; pain with resisted wrist flexion and pronation."
  - "Posterior fat pad sign = effusion = occult fracture until proven otherwise."
  - "Pediatric elbow ossification order: **CRITOE**: Capitellum, Radial head, Internal (medial) epicondyle, Trochlea, Olecranon, External (lateral) epicondyle."
  - "Posterior dislocation is the most common elbow dislocation."
imaging:
  xray: >-
    AP and **true lateral with the elbow at 90°**. Look for: **fat pads** (a visible posterior fat pad or elevated
    anterior "sail" fat pad = effusion); the **anterior humeral line** (through the middle third of the capitellum);
    the **radiocapitellar line** (through the capitellum in all views). In children, interpret ossification centers
    using **CRITOE**.
  ct: Complex fractures (terrible triad, distal humerus intra-articular fractures).
  mri: >-
    UCL and LCL tears, epicondylitis (common tendon thickening and increased signal), osteochondral lesions (e.g.
    osteochondritis dissecans of the capitellum in young throwers).
  ultrasound: >-
    Common extensor/flexor tendinopathy (hypoechoic thickening, neovascularity on Doppler), UCL assessment with
    **dynamic valgus stress**, effusion, olecranon bursitis, and ulnar nerve subluxation.
  keyViews:
    - "Elbow AP and lateral at 90°"
    - "Radial head–capitellum view (oblique) for subtle radial head fractures"
exam:
  landmarks: >-
    Medial and lateral epicondyles, olecranon (straight line in extension, isosceles triangle in 90° flexion), radial
    head (distal to the lateral epicondyle, rotates with pronation/supination), ulnar nerve in the groove behind the
    medial epicondyle.
  palpation: Palpate both epicondyles, the radial head, the olecranon and bursa, and the distal biceps tendon.
  testing: Flexion, extension, pronation and supination (active and passive). Varus and valgus stress at about 20–30° of flexion.
  specialTests:
    - name: Cozen test
      technique: Elbow extended (or slightly flexed), forearm pronated. The patient makes a fist and extends and radially deviates the wrist against resistance.
      positive: Pain at the lateral epicondyle.
      significance: Lateral epicondylitis.
    - name: Mill test
      technique: With the elbow extended and forearm pronated, the examiner passively flexes the wrist fully.
      positive: Pain at the lateral epicondyle.
      significance: Lateral epicondylitis.
    - name: Golfer's elbow test
      technique: Elbow extended. The patient flexes the wrist and pronates the forearm against resistance.
      positive: Pain at the medial epicondyle.
      significance: Medial epicondylitis.
    - name: Valgus stress test
      technique: Elbow flexed about 20–30° to unlock the olecranon. The examiner applies a valgus force.
      positive: Medial pain and/or increased laxity compared with the other side.
      significance: UCL sprain or tear.
quiz:
  - stem: >-
      A 45-year-old carpenter has lateral elbow pain for 2 months. Pain is reproduced by resisted wrist extension with
      the elbow extended and forearm pronated. Which tendon is most commonly involved?
    choices:
      - Extensor carpi ulnaris
      - Extensor carpi radialis brevis
      - Flexor carpi radialis
      - Pronator teres
      - Brachioradialis
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      **Lateral epicondylitis** is a tendinopathy of the common extensor origin, most characteristically **ECRB**.
      Pronator teres and FCR are involved in medial epicondylitis.
  - stem: A lateral radiograph of a child's elbow shows an elevated anterior fat pad and a visible posterior fat pad. What does this indicate?
    choices:
      - Normal finding
      - Joint effusion, suggesting an occult fracture
      - Olecranon bursitis
      - Ulnar collateral ligament tear
      - Nursemaid's elbow
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      A **visible posterior fat pad** is never normal. It indicates an effusion, which after trauma suggests an occult
      fracture (supracondylar in children, radial head in adults). Nursemaid's elbow has normal radiographs.
  - stem: Which ligament is the primary restraint to valgus stress at the elbow in an overhead throwing athlete?
    choices:
      - Anular ligament
      - Radial collateral ligament
      - Anterior band of the ulnar collateral ligament
      - Quadrate ligament
      - Interosseous membrane
    answer: C
    explanation: >-
      The **anterior band of the UCL** is the key restraint to valgus stress and is injured by repetitive throwing.
