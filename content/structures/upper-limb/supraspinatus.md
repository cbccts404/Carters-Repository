name: Supraspinatus
type: muscle
region: upper-limb
subregion: Pectoral & scapular region
taName: Musculus supraspinatus
summary: Rotator cuff muscle in the supraspinous fossa that initiates abduction and is the most commonly torn cuff tendon.
tags: [rotator-cuff, shoulder]
systems: [musculoskeletal]
highYield: true
status: draft
anatomy:
  location: Supraspinous fossa of the scapula, passing under the acromion to the top of the humeral head.
  group: Rotator cuff (SITS = Supraspinatus, Infraspinatus, Teres minor, Subscapularis); scapulohumeral muscles.
  origin: Supraspinous fossa of the [[scapula]] and the deep fascia covering it.
  insertion: Superior facet of the greater tubercle of the [[humerus]].
  innervation: "Suprascapular nerve (C5, C6) {{verify: some texts include C4}}"
  bloodSupply: "Suprascapular artery {{verify}}"
  action:
    - Initiates abduction of the arm (roughly the first 15°); assists deltoid through the rest of abduction
    - With the other rotator cuff muscles, holds the humeral head in the glenoid cavity
  relations: >-
    The tendon passes beneath the **coracoacromial arch** (acromion, coracoacromial ligament, coracoid process). The
    **subacromial (subdeltoid) bursa** separates it from the arch and deltoid. It blends with the superior capsule of
    the [[glenohumeral-joint]]. The suprascapular nerve reaches the muscle by passing through the suprascapular notch
    beneath the superior transverse scapular ligament.
clinical:
  - title: Rotator cuff tear
    highYield: true
    mechanism: >-
      Supraspinatus is the most commonly torn cuff tendon. Tears are usually degenerative in patients over ~40
      (attrition under the arch, reduced vascularity near the insertion) and are sometimes acute (fall, lifting, or
      dislocation in older patients).
    presentation: >-
      Lateral shoulder pain, often at night and when lying on the affected side. Weak abduction and external rotation.
      Full-thickness tears may not allow the patient to lower the arm smoothly from abduction (**drop-arm sign**).
      Passive ROM is typically preserved, unlike adhesive capsulitis.
  - title: Subacromial impingement / rotator cuff tendinopathy
    highYield: true
    mechanism: >-
      Irritation of the supraspinatus tendon and subacromial bursa beneath the coracoacromial arch with repetitive
      overhead activity (painters, swimmers, throwers). Contributors include acromial shape, AC osteophytes and poor
      scapular mechanics.
    presentation: >-
      A **painful arc** between about 60° and 120° of abduction. Pain with overhead activity. Positive Neer and
      Hawkins–Kennedy signs. Pain with the empty-can test, with preserved strength if there's no tear.
  - title: Calcific tendinitis
    highYield: false
    mechanism: Deposition of calcium hydroxyapatite within the tendon, most often supraspinatus.
    presentation: Acute, severe shoulder pain with markedly restricted motion during the resorptive phase; calcification visible on X-ray.
pance:
  - "Most commonly torn rotator cuff tendon: **supraspinatus**."
  - "**Empty-can (Jobe) test** isolates supraspinatus; **drop-arm test** suggests a full-thickness cuff tear."
  - "**Painful arc** (60–120°) and **Neer/Hawkins** signs suggest subacromial impingement."
  - "Cuff pathology: passive ROM preserved, active ROM limited. Adhesive capsulitis: both active and passive ROM limited."
  - "MRI (or ultrasound) is used to confirm and grade rotator cuff tears."
imaging:
  xray: >-
    Often normal. Look for calcific tendinitis (calcification above the greater tuberosity), a hooked acromion or AC
    osteophytes (outlet view), and changes in the greater tuberosity. In chronic massive tears the humeral head rides
    up and the **acromiohumeral interval narrows**. {{verify: a threshold of <7 mm is commonly quoted}}
  mri: >-
    Oblique coronal T2 fat-saturated images show the tendon from the muscle belly to the footprint. A full-thickness
    tear appears as a **fluid-bright gap** through the full tendon thickness, with or without retraction. Oblique
    sagittal images assess **fatty infiltration and atrophy** of the muscle belly, which predicts outcome after repair.
    MR arthrography: contrast leaking into the subacromial–subdeltoid bursa indicates a full-thickness tear.
  ultrasound: >-
    Normal tendon: a hyperechoic, fibrillar "beak" inserting on the greater tuberosity beneath the deltoid and bursa,
    best seen with the arm extended and internally rotated (hand in back pocket). Full-thickness tear: a hypo- or
    anechoic defect, **non-visualization** of the tendon, or loss of the normal convex bursal contour. Dynamic
    scanning can show impingement.
  keyViews:
    - "Shoulder AP (internal and external rotation), scapular Y/outlet, axillary"
    - "MRI oblique coronal and oblique sagittal T2 fat-sat"
    - "Ultrasound long and short axis with the arm in the modified Crass (hand-in-back-pocket) position"
exam:
  landmarks: >-
    The muscle belly lies in the supraspinous fossa above the spine of the scapula (atrophy appears as hollowing). The
    tendon insertion can be palpated just anterior/inferior to the anterolateral acromion when the shoulder is extended.
  palpation: >-
    Extend and internally rotate the arm (hand behind the back) to bring the insertion out from under the acromion,
    then palpate for tenderness below the anterolateral acromion.
  testing: Resisted abduction in the scapular plane. Inspect for supraspinous fossa atrophy.
  specialTests:
    - name: Empty-can (Jobe) test
      technique: >-
        Arms abducted to 90° in the scapular plane (about 30° anterior to the coronal plane) and fully internally
        rotated (thumbs pointing down). The patient resists downward pressure.
      positive: Pain and/or weakness compared with the other side.
      significance: Supraspinatus tendinopathy (pain) or tear (weakness).
    - name: Drop-arm test
      technique: >-
        The examiner passively abducts the arm to about 90°, and the patient slowly lowers it to the side.
      positive: The arm drops suddenly or can't be lowered smoothly.
      significance: Suggests a full-thickness rotator cuff tear.
    - name: Painful arc
      technique: The patient actively abducts the arm through its full range.
      positive: Pain between about 60° and 120°, easing above and below that range.
      significance: Subacromial impingement / cuff tendinopathy.
    - name: Neer and Hawkins–Kennedy impingement signs
      technique: >-
        **Neer:** stabilize the scapula and passively forward-flex the internally rotated arm fully. **Hawkins–Kennedy:**
        forward-flex the shoulder and elbow to 90°, then passively internally rotate the shoulder.
      positive: Pain in the anterolateral shoulder.
      significance: Subacromial impingement.
quiz:
  - stem: >-
      A 58-year-old house painter has 4 months of right lateral shoulder pain that wakes him at night. Pain occurs
      between 70° and 110° of active abduction. Abduction against resistance with the arm internally rotated and the
      thumb down is painful and weak. Passive range of motion is full. Which muscle is most likely involved?
    choices:
      - Infraspinatus
      - Supraspinatus
      - Subscapularis
      - Teres minor
      - Deltoid
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      A painful arc and a painful, weak **empty-can test** indicate **supraspinatus** tendinopathy or tear. Full passive
      ROM argues against adhesive capsulitis. Infraspinatus and teres minor are tested by resisted external rotation,
      subscapularis by lift-off/belly-press.
  - stem: Supraspinatus is innervated by a nerve arising from which part of the brachial plexus?
    choices:
      - Roots
      - Superior trunk
      - Lateral cord
      - Posterior cord
      - Medial cord
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      The **suprascapular nerve** arises from the **superior trunk** (C5–C6) and supplies supraspinatus and
      infraspinatus. It passes through the suprascapular notch below the superior transverse scapular ligament.
  - stem: >-
      An examiner passively abducts a patient's arm to 90° and asks her to lower it slowly. The arm falls abruptly to
      her side. What is the most likely diagnosis?
    choices:
      - Adhesive capsulitis
      - Full-thickness rotator cuff tear
      - Acromioclavicular joint separation
      - Biceps tendinopathy
      - Axillary nerve palsy
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      A positive **drop-arm test** suggests a **full-thickness rotator cuff tear**, usually involving supraspinatus.
      Adhesive capsulitis limits passive motion rather than causing the arm to drop.
  - stem: A patient with a complete supraspinatus tear would have the most difficulty with which movement?
    choices:
      - Initiating abduction from the side
      - Medial rotation of the arm
      - Elbow flexion
      - Shoulder extension
      - Scapular retraction
    answer: A
    explanation: >-
      Supraspinatus **initiates abduction** (about the first 15°) and keeps the humeral head seated so deltoid can
      continue abduction. Patients often shrug or lean to start the movement.
