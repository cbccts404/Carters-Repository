taName: Scalpus
summary: Five-layered soft tissue covering the skull vault. Its rich blood supply makes lacerations bleed heavily, and its loose areolar "danger layer" lets infection spread into the skull.
tags: [scalp, trauma]
highYield: false
status: draft
anatomy:
  location: Over the cranial vault from the eyebrows to the superior nuchal lines, and laterally to the zygomatic arches.
  parts:
    - "**S — Skin**: thick, hair-bearing, with many sebaceous glands"
    - "**C — Connective tissue (dense)**: contains the vessels and nerves; fibrous septa hold vessel walls open, so cut vessels gape and **bleed profusely**"
    - "**A — Aponeurosis (galea aponeurotica)**: joins the frontal and occipital bellies of occipitofrontalis"
    - "**L — Loose areolar tissue**: the **\"danger layer\"**; allows the upper three layers to move, and blood or pus spreads freely within it (subgaleal hemorrhage)"
    - "**P — Pericranium**: periosteum of the skull, attached at the sutures (limits cephalohematoma to one bone)"
  bloodSupply: >-
    Branches of the **external carotid** (superficial temporal, posterior auricular, occipital) and the **internal
    carotid** via the ophthalmic artery (supratrochlear, supraorbital); extensive anastomoses.
  venousDrainage: Veins accompanying the arteries; **emissary veins** in the danger layer connect scalp veins with the intracranial dural venous sinuses.
  lymphatics: Occipital, posterior auricular (mastoid), preauricular (parotid) and submandibular nodes (see [[cervical-lymph-nodes]]).
  innervation: Anteriorly branches of the trigeminal nerve (supratrochlear, supraorbital, zygomaticotemporal, auriculotemporal); posteriorly the greater occipital nerve (C2) ([[greater-occipital-nerve]]), lesser occipital and great auricular nerves ([[cervical-plexus]]) and third occipital nerve.
  relations: The first three layers are firmly bound together and move as one ("scalp proper").
clinical:
  - title: Scalp lacerations
    highYield: true
    mechanism: The dense connective tissue holds vessels open.
    presentation: Heavy bleeding (significant blood loss possible in children and anticoagulated patients). Control with direct pressure and closure; repair a gaping galea to restore frontalis function and hemostasis.
  - title: Subgaleal hemorrhage and cephalohematoma
    highYield: true
    mechanism: '**Subgaleal** bleeding (in the loose areolar layer), e.g. after vacuum-assisted delivery, can spread over the whole vault; **cephalohematoma** is subperiosteal, so it is **limited by suture lines**.'
    presentation: Subgaleal hemorrhage — a fluctuant swelling crossing sutures that can cause hypovolemic shock in newborns. Cephalohematoma — a firm swelling over one bone appearing hours after birth, risk of jaundice. Caput succedaneum — scalp edema crossing sutures, present at birth, resolves in days.
  - title: Spread of infection via emissary veins
    highYield: false
    mechanism: Infection in the danger layer can travel through valveless emissary veins to the dural venous sinuses.
    presentation: Meningitis or venous sinus thrombosis after scalp infection (rare).
pance:
  - "Scalp layers: **S**kin, **C**onnective tissue, **A**poneurosis, **L**oose areolar, **P**ericranium."
  - "Cephalohematoma **doesn't cross sutures**; caput succedaneum and subgaleal hemorrhage **do**."
  - "Subgaleal hemorrhage in a newborn can cause **shock**."
imaging:
  ct: Head CT shows subgaleal hematoma and underlying fractures after trauma.
  ultrasound: Distinguishes scalp collections in neonates.
exam:
  palpation: In a newborn, a swelling limited to one bone and not crossing sutures = cephalohematoma; a boggy swelling crossing sutures = caput or subgaleal hemorrhage (monitor hemoglobin and head circumference).
  testing: Explore lacerations for galeal tears and underlying fracture.

    answer: B
    explanation: >-
