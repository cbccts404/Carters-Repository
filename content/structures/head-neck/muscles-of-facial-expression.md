---
name: Muscles of facial expression
type: muscle
region: head-neck
subregion: Face & scalp
taName: Musculi faciei
aka: [Mimetic muscles]
summary: Thin subcutaneous muscles derived from the second pharyngeal arch and supplied by the facial nerve (CN VII). Their pattern of weakness distinguishes a peripheral facial palsy (Bell palsy) from a stroke.
tags: [face, facial-nerve]
systems: [neurologic]
highYield: true
status: draft
anatomy:
  location: In the superficial fascia of the face, scalp and neck; most insert into the skin.
  group: Second pharyngeal arch derivatives
  origin: Mostly from bones of the face or fascia (e.g. orbicularis oculi from the medial orbital margin; zygomaticus major from the zygomatic bone; buccinator from the alveolar processes and pterygomandibular raphe).
  insertion: Mostly into the skin and each other (e.g. modiolus at the angle of the mouth), which is why they move the skin.
  innervation: >-
    **[[facial-nerve|Facial nerve (CN VII)]]** — its five terminal branches leave the [[parotid-gland]]: **temporal, zygomatic, buccal,
    marginal mandibular and cervical** ("To Zanzibar By Motor Car"). Also stapedius, stylohyoid and the posterior
    belly of digastric.
  bloodSupply: Facial, superficial temporal and transverse facial arteries (branches of the [[external-carotid-artery]]).
  action:
    - "**Orbicularis oculi**: closes the eyelids (blinking, tight closure)"
    - "**Frontalis** (occipitofrontalis): raises the eyebrows, wrinkles the forehead"
    - "**Orbicularis oris**: closes and purses the lips"
    - "**Buccinator**: compresses the cheek (keeps food between the teeth; blowing)"
    - "**Zygomaticus major**: smiling; **depressor anguli oris**: frowning"
    - "**Platysma**: tenses the skin of the neck, depresses the mandible"
  relations: >-
    The upper face (frontalis, upper orbicularis oculi) receives **bilateral** cortical input, whereas the lower face
    receives mainly contralateral input — the key to localizing facial weakness.
clinical:
  - title: Bell palsy (peripheral facial nerve palsy)
    highYield: true
    mechanism: Idiopathic (often attributed to HSV reactivation) swelling of the facial nerve in the facial canal — a **lower motor neuron** lesion.
    presentation: >-
      Sudden unilateral weakness of the **whole face, including the forehead** (can't raise the eyebrow or close the
      eye), drooping mouth, loss of nasolabial fold; may have hyperacusis, loss of taste to the anterior two-thirds of
      the tongue, and decreased tearing. Treat with **oral corticosteroids within 72 hours** (± antivirals) and **eye
      protection**.
  - title: Central (upper motor neuron) facial palsy
    highYield: true
    mechanism: Stroke or tumor affecting the corticobulbar fibers above the facial nucleus.
    presentation: Weakness of the **lower face only on the opposite side**, with the **forehead spared** (bilateral upper-face innervation), usually with other deficits (arm weakness, aphasia).
  - title: Other causes of peripheral facial palsy
    highYield: true
    mechanism: '**Ramsay Hunt syndrome** (VZV — vesicles in the ear canal), **Lyme disease** (may be bilateral), parotid tumors or surgery, temporal bone fracture, otitis media, sarcoidosis.'
    presentation: Look in the ear for vesicles, ask about tick exposure, and palpate the parotid; bilateral palsy suggests Lyme, Guillain–Barré or sarcoidosis.
pance:
  - "**Forehead involved** = peripheral (Bell); **forehead spared** = central (stroke)."
  - "Bell palsy → **prednisone within 72 h** + eye care (lubricant, taping)."
  - "Vesicles in the ear + facial palsy → **Ramsay Hunt** (VZV)."
  - "Bilateral facial palsy → Lyme, Guillain–Barré, sarcoidosis."
  - "Facial nerve branches: Temporal, Zygomatic, Buccal, Marginal mandibular, Cervical."
imaging:
  ct: Head CT if stroke is suspected (forehead-sparing weakness, other deficits); temporal bone CT for trauma.
  mri: MRI with contrast for atypical or progressive palsy, recurrence, or no recovery after about 3 months (tumor along the nerve, e.g. parotid or cerebellopontine angle).
exam:
  landmarks: The facial nerve branches fan out from the anterior border of the parotid gland.
  testing: >-
    Ask the patient to raise the eyebrows (frontalis), close the eyes tightly against resistance (orbicularis oculi),
    smile and show the teeth, puff the cheeks (buccinator), and tense the neck (platysma). Look for **Bell phenomenon**
    (the eye rolls up when trying to close). Check taste, hearing (hyperacusis) and the ear canal.
quiz:
  - stem: >-
      A 35-year-old woman wakes with drooping of the right side of her face. She cannot raise her right eyebrow or fully
      close her right eye. There are no other neurologic deficits and the ear canal is normal. What is the best
      treatment?
    choices:
      - Immediate tPA
      - Oral corticosteroids started within 72 hours and eye protection
      - Surgical decompression of the facial nerve
      - IV acyclovir alone
      - No treatment; reassure only
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      Involvement of the **forehead** indicates a **peripheral (lower motor neuron)** lesion — **Bell palsy**.
      **Corticosteroids within 72 hours** improve recovery; protect the eye from exposure keratitis. tPA is for ischemic
      stroke, which spares the forehead.
  - stem: Why is the forehead spared in a stroke causing facial weakness?
    choices:
      - The frontalis is supplied by the trigeminal nerve
      - The upper face receives bilateral corticobulbar input
      - The facial nucleus is not affected by strokes
      - The frontalis has no motor innervation
      - The lower face receives bilateral input
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      The part of the facial nucleus supplying the **upper face receives input from both cerebral hemispheres**, so a
      unilateral cortical lesion spares the forehead. The lower face receives mainly contralateral input.
