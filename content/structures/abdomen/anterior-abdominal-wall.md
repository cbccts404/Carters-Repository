summary: Layered musculoaponeurotic wall from the costal margin to the pelvis. Its planes and regions are the map for describing abdominal pain, and it is the site of ventral hernias.
tags: [abdominal-wall, hernia, exam]
systems: [gastrointestinal, musculoskeletal]
highYield: true
status: draft
anatomy:
  location: Between the xiphoid process and costal margins above and the iliac crests, inguinal ligaments and pubic bones below.
  boundaries:
    superior: Xiphoid process and costal margins (7th–10th costal cartilages)
    inferior: Iliac crests, inguinal ligaments, pubic crests and pubic symphysis
    lateral: Mid-axillary lines (continuous with the posterolateral wall)
  contents:
    - "**Layers, superficial to deep**: skin; superficial fascia with a fatty layer (Camper) and a membranous layer (Scarpa); **external oblique**, **internal oblique**, **transversus abdominis**; **transversalis fascia**; extraperitoneal fat; **parietal peritoneum**"
    - "[[rectus-abdominis]] within the rectus sheath on each side of the **linea alba**"
    - "Thoracoabdominal nerves (**T7–T11**), subcostal nerve (T12), iliohypogastric and ilioinguinal nerves (L1)"
    - "Superior and [[inferior-epigastric-artery|inferior epigastric]] vessels in the rectus sheath; superficial epigastric vessels in the subcutaneous tissue"
  communications: Continuous inferiorly with the [[inguinal-canal]], which passes obliquely through its lower part.
  notes: >-
    **Surface regions.** Four **quadrants** (RUQ, LUQ, RLQ, LLQ) divided by the median plane and the transumbilical
    plane, or nine **regions** divided by the midclavicular lines, the subcostal plane and the transtubercular plane
    (epigastric, umbilical, hypogastric/pubic in the middle; hypochondriac, lumbar/flank, inguinal/iliac on each side).
    **Landmarks**: the **umbilicus** lies at about the **L3/L4** disc and the **T10** dermatome. The **transpyloric
    plane (L1)**, halfway between the jugular notch and the pubic symphysis, passes roughly through the pylorus, the
    fundus of the gallbladder (9th costal cartilage), the neck of the pancreas, the hila of the kidneys and the
    duodenojejunal flexure. The **linea semilunaris** marks the lateral edge of the rectus sheath; the **arcuate line**
    (roughly one-third of the way from the umbilicus to the pubis) marks where the posterior rectus sheath ends.
    {{verify: vertebral levels vary with body habitus and posture}}
clinical:
  - title: Ventral hernias (umbilical, epigastric, incisional, Spigelian)
    highYield: true
    mechanism: >-
      Protrusion through a weakness in the wall. **Umbilical**: common in infants (most close spontaneously in early
      childhood) and in adults with raised intra-abdominal pressure (obesity, pregnancy, ascites). **Epigastric**:
      through the linea alba above the umbilicus. **Incisional**: through a surgical scar. **Spigelian**: through the
      semilunar line, usually near the arcuate line.
    presentation: A bulge that enlarges with coughing or straining. Pain, tenderness, irreducibility or signs of obstruction suggest incarceration or strangulation, a surgical emergency.
  - title: Referred and localized abdominal pain
    highYield: true
    mechanism: >-
      **Visceral** pain (autonomic afferents) is poorly localized to the midline by embryologic origin: **foregut →
      epigastric**, **midgut → periumbilical**, **hindgut → suprapubic**. **Parietal** peritoneal pain (somatic nerves
      of the wall) is sharp and well localized.
    presentation: "For example, appendicitis begins as periumbilical pain (visceral), then localizes to the RLQ once the parietal peritoneum is inflamed."
  - title: Abdominal wall signs of intra-abdominal bleeding
    highYield: true
    mechanism: Blood tracking from the retroperitoneum or peritoneal cavity into the subcutaneous tissue.
    presentation: >-
      **Cullen sign** (periumbilical ecchymosis) and **Grey Turner sign** (flank ecchymosis), e.g. in severe hemorrhagic
      pancreatitis or ruptured ectopic pregnancy. **Caput medusae** (dilated periumbilical veins) indicates portal
      hypertension.
pance:
  - "Visceral pain: foregut → epigastrium, midgut → periumbilical, hindgut → suprapubic."
  - "Umbilicus = **T10** dermatome, about **L3/L4**."
  - "Cullen (periumbilical) and Grey Turner (flank) ecchymosis → hemorrhagic pancreatitis, ruptured ectopic."

imaging:
  xray: Limited. Free intraperitoneal air under the diaphragm on an upright CXR; bowel gas within a hernia sac.
  ct: CT defines hernia contents, the neck of the defect, incarceration/strangulation, abdominal wall masses, hematomas and abscesses.
  ultrasound: >-
    Dynamic ultrasound with Valsalva identifies ventral hernias and their contents at the bedside, distinguishes
    hernias from lipomas or hematomas, and guides abdominal wall blocks (e.g. the TAP block in the plane between
    internal oblique and transversus abdominis).
  keyViews:
    - "CT abdomen/pelvis with contrast"
    - "Ultrasound over the bulge at rest and with Valsalva"
exam:
  landmarks: Xiphoid, costal margins, umbilicus, anterior superior iliac spines, pubic tubercles, linea alba, linea semilunaris.
  palpation: >-
    Order of the abdominal exam: **inspection → auscultation → percussion → palpation** (auscultate before palpation
    so bowel sounds aren't altered). Palpate lightly then deeply, starting away from the painful area. Check for
    hernias with the patient coughing or lifting the head.
  testing: >-
    Look for guarding, rigidity and rebound tenderness (peritonitis). Tensing the wall (head or legs raised) makes
    diastasis recti and ventral hernias more obvious.
  specialTests:
    - name: Carnett sign
      technique: Locate the point of maximal tenderness, then have the patient tense the abdominal muscles (lift the head and shoulders or both legs) while you press again.
      positive: Tenderness stays the same or increases with the muscles tensed.
      significance: Suggests an abdominal **wall** source (e.g. nerve entrapment, hernia, hematoma). Visceral tenderness usually decreases because the tense muscles shield the viscera.
quiz:
  - stem: A 19-year-old man has vague periumbilical pain that, over 12 hours, becomes sharp and localized to the right lower quadrant. What explains the shift?
    choices:
      - Rupture of the appendix into the peritoneal cavity
      - Visceral afferent pain followed by parietal peritoneal irritation
      - Referred pain via the phrenic nerve
      - Spread of infection to the rectus sheath
      - Compression of the ilioinguinal nerve
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      Early appendiceal distension stimulates **visceral afferents** entering at about T10, giving poorly localized
      periumbilical pain (midgut). When inflammation reaches the **parietal peritoneum** (somatic innervation), the pain
      becomes sharp and localized to the RLQ.
  - stem: Which dermatome corresponds to the umbilicus?
    choices:
      - T4
      - T7
      - T10
      - L1
      - L3
    answer: C
    explanation: The umbilicus lies in the **T10** dermatome. T4 is the nipple; L1 is the inguinal region.
  - stem: >-
      A patient has point tenderness in the right lower abdominal wall. When she lifts her head and shoulders off the
      table, the tenderness increases. What does this suggest?
    choices:
      - Acute appendicitis
      - A source in the abdominal wall
      - Ovarian torsion
      - Ureteral stone
      - Psoas abscess
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      A positive **Carnett sign** (tenderness unchanged or worse with the wall tensed) points to an **abdominal wall**
      source such as anterior cutaneous nerve entrapment or a hernia. Visceral pain typically lessens.
  - stem: >-
      A patient with severe hemorrhagic pancreatitis develops bruising around the umbilicus. What is this sign called?
    choices:
      - Grey Turner sign
      - Cullen sign
      - Caput medusae
      - Carnett sign
      - Murphy sign
    answer: B
    explanation: >-
      **Cullen sign** is **periumbilical** ecchymosis; **Grey Turner sign** is **flank** ecchymosis. Both reflect blood
      tracking from the retroperitoneum or peritoneal cavity, e.g. in hemorrhagic pancreatitis or ruptured ectopic
      pregnancy. Caput medusae is dilated periumbilical veins in portal hypertension.
  - stem: >-
      A hernia protrudes through the lateral edge of the rectus sheath in the lower abdomen, near the arcuate line. What
      type of hernia is it?
    choices:
      - Epigastric hernia
      - Umbilical hernia
      - Direct inguinal hernia
      - Femoral hernia
      - Spigelian hernia
    answer: E
    explanation: >-
      A **Spigelian hernia** passes through the **linea semilunaris** (the lateral edge of the rectus sheath), usually
      near the arcuate line. Epigastric hernias pass through the linea alba above the umbilicus.
