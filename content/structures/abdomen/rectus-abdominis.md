name: Rectus abdominis
type: muscle
region: abdomen
subregion: Abdominal wall
taName: Musculus rectus abdominis
summary: Long strap muscle on either side of the linea alba, enclosed in the rectus sheath. It flexes the trunk, and hematoma in its sheath is a classic complication of anticoagulation.
tags: [abdominal-wall]
systems: [musculoskeletal, gastrointestinal]
highYield: false
status: draft
anatomy:
  location: Anterior abdominal wall, on each side of the linea alba, within the rectus sheath.
  group: Anterolateral abdominal wall muscles (vertical group, with pyramidalis).
  origin: Pubic symphysis and pubic crest.
  insertion: Xiphoid process and the 5th–7th costal cartilages.
  innervation: Thoracoabdominal nerves (anterior rami of T7–T11) and subcostal nerve (T12).
  bloodSupply: "Superior epigastric artery (from the internal thoracic artery) and [[inferior-epigastric-artery]] (from the external iliac artery), which anastomose within the sheath."
  action:
    - Flexes the trunk (lumbar vertebral column)
    - Compresses the abdominal viscera and increases intra-abdominal pressure
    - Stabilizes the pelvis (e.g. during walking)
  relations: >-
    Usually three **tendinous intersections** attach it to the anterior layer of the sheath (the "six-pack"). **Rectus
    sheath**: **above the arcuate line**, the anterior layer is formed by the external oblique aponeurosis and the
    anterior lamina of the internal oblique aponeurosis, and the posterior layer by the posterior lamina of the internal
    oblique and the transversus abdominis aponeurosis. **Below the arcuate line**, all three aponeuroses pass
    **anterior** to the muscle, leaving only transversalis fascia behind it. The inferior epigastric vessels enter the
    sheath near the arcuate line.
clinical:
  - title: Rectus sheath hematoma
    highYield: true
    mechanism: >-
      Rupture of the epigastric vessels or muscle fibers, typically in **anticoagulated** or older patients, often
      triggered by coughing, straining, trauma or injections. Below the arcuate line there is no posterior sheath, so
      the hematoma can spread and become large.
    presentation: >-
      Acute abdominal pain with a **tender, non-pulsatile abdominal wall mass** that doesn't cross the midline and
      remains palpable when the muscles are tensed (**Fothergill sign**). Possible anemia.
  - title: Diastasis recti
    highYield: false
    mechanism: Widening of the linea alba between the two recti, common after pregnancy and in infants. It is not a true hernia (no fascial defect).
    presentation: A midline ridge that bulges when the patient lifts the head from supine. Usually managed conservatively.
pance:
  - "Rectus sheath hematoma: anticoagulated patient + coughing → tender abdominal wall mass; confirm with CT or ultrasound."
  - "**Below the arcuate line** there is no posterior rectus sheath (only transversalis fascia)."
  - "Diastasis recti is not a hernia; there is no fascial defect."
imaging:
  ct: CT is the test of choice for rectus sheath hematoma (a lenticular or spindle-shaped mass within the sheath; active extravasation on contrast).
  ultrasound: Shows the muscle, sheath and hematoma. Measures inter-rectus distance in diastasis recti.
  keyViews:
    - "CT abdomen with contrast (hematoma)"
exam:
  landmarks: The lateral border of the muscle forms the **linea semilunaris**, which crosses the costal margin at the tip of the 9th costal cartilage (the gallbladder fundus point on the right).
  palpation: Palpate for a mass in the wall; ask the patient to tense the abdomen to see whether it stays palpable (wall) or disappears (intra-abdominal).
  testing: Trunk flexion (sit-up) against resistance. Observe for midline bulging (diastasis) on head lift.

      **Rectus sheath hematoma** is a classic complication in anticoagulated patients after coughing. A mass that
      remains palpable with the muscles tensed (Fothergill sign) lies in the wall, not the peritoneal cavity. Confirm with CT.

    answer: C
    explanation: >-
      Below the arcuate line all three aponeuroses pass **anterior** to the muscle, so only **transversalis fascia**
